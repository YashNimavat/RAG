# P3: Keyword search, TF-IDF and BM25

**What this is:** the short, book-first reading for P3. It explains how keyword search scores a chunk: three counts (tf, df and idf), then TF-IDF, then BM25 exactly as the `rank_bm25` library computes it, and why the tokenizer decides whether an exact term can match at all.
**What this is not:** the full lesson with the lab ([concepts/P3_bm25.md](../../concepts/P3_bm25.md)), or how to merge BM25 with dense search ([R1](R1_hybrid_fusion.md)).

**The answer first.** Keyword search (BM25) scores a chunk by the words it shares with your question. A shared word adds more when few chunks contain it, a little more for each repeat (with fast-shrinking returns), and a bit more in a short chunk than in a long one. BM25 knows nothing about meaning: a synonym scores zero. And it matches **tokens**, not letters: with the repo's `split()`, the question word `BECCS` never matches the text's `(BECCS),`, so the right chunk scores 0. With a tokenizer that lower-cases and strips punctuation, BM25 puts that chunk first. Dense retrieval puts the same chunk only 4th (on the 97 chunks defined below).

> **Read in the book:** Ch12 p.1–2/14, p.5–6/14, p.8/14, p.10/14, p.12/14 and p.14/14, then the glossary entries in App p.14/23, p.17/23, p.18/23 and p.22/23. About 15 minutes. Read them after §1. Optional: the index analogy in Ch12 p.3/14, and Ch13 p.5/13, where BM25 feeds a reranker.

**Labels used below.** *Toy* numbers come from small made-up corpora, mostly P3's six documents. *Demonstration* means a hand-picked query on the repo's climate PDF; it is not an evaluation. Two different chunkings of that PDF appear, and every number says which one it uses:
- **91 windows**: P3's plain 1,000-character slices with 200 characters of overlap (the P3 lab);
- **97 chunks**: the C-lab index that R1 also uses (07's recursive chunker, per page).

Every number comes from `p3_measure.py` and `p3_kit_measure.py` ([P3_FACTS](../../sources/verification/P3/P3_FACTS.md)), or from R1's run on the 97 chunks ([R1_FACTS](../../sources/verification/R1/R1_FACTS.md)). No API was called.

## 1. The failure keyword search fixes

**The setup.** The book opens its fusion chapter with a chemical acronym (Ch12 p.1/14). Meaning-based search ranks the one chunk that names it fourth, behind chunks that only discuss the general topic. R1 ran five exact-term queries on the 97 chunks, with both retrievers, and recorded where the chunk that contains the term lands.

**The result** (demonstration, 97 chunks):

```
query                           dense rank   BM25 rank (lower-cased tokens)   BM25 rank (repo's split())
"IPCC"                              3                 1                            not found
"What does BECCS stand for?"        4                 1                            not found
"methane CH4"                       4                 1                            2
"Kyoto Protocol 1997"               1                 1                            1
"limit warming to 1.5 degrees"      4                 1                            1
```

**What this means.** Dense retrieval turns the whole question into one point of meaning, so a rare acronym is blurred into its topic and the chunk that *names* it slips down. BM25 compares words. When the rare word is in a chunk, that chunk stands out. But look at the right-hand column: with the wrong tokenizer, BM25 finds nothing at all for two of the five.

**For your project:** BM25 is the retriever for identifiers: acronyms, codes, version strings, years. Your eval set has an `exact-term` question category for exactly this (06 §6.2), and the v2.0 experiment targets it (06 §4.2).

We follow one question through the rest of this reading: **"What does BECCS stand for?"**, on the 91 windows. The PDF mentions BECCS once, inside "bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS)".

## 2. Three counts before any formula

BM25 needs only three counts. Learn them in plain words first.

- **How often a word appears inside one chunk.** This is the *term frequency* (tf), written f.
- **How many chunks contain the word at least once.** This is the *document frequency* (df), written n. It is counted over the whole collection.
- **A weight that is high when few chunks contain the word.** This is the *inverse document frequency* (idf). A word found everywhere tells you nothing about a chunk; a word found in two chunks points straight at them.

**The setup.** The six-document toy. Document d0 is "BPA regulation (2019): the EU limits BPA in food-contact plastics." Here are the counts for three words.

**The result** (toy):

```
word       tf in d0     df (of 6 docs)     so its weight is
eu            1               1             highest (the rarest word)
bpa           2               2             middling
climate       0               4             low (most documents have it)
```

And the BECCS question on the 91 windows:

```
token     df (of 91 windows)
what            0          ← in no window: adds nothing
does            0
stand           0
beccs           2          ← one mention, counted twice (see below)
for            80          ← almost everywhere
```

**What this means.** Three of the five question words occur nowhere in the PDF, so they contribute nothing and cause no error. `beccs` is the word that will carry the score. `for` is in 80 of the 91 windows, so it should be worth very little. Why does `beccs` have df 2 when the PDF says it once? The mention sits in the 200 characters where windows 82 and 83 overlap, so both windows contain it.

**The tempting wrong idea:** "idf counts how often the word appears in the chunk." That is tf. The idf uses df, a count across the whole collection.

**For your project:** chunking changes the counts. With 200-character overlap, 184 of the 799 words that occur only once in the PDF (23.0%) are counted in two windows.

## 3. TF-IDF, and why counting every repeat is a problem

**The setup.** The textbook combination multiplies the two ideas (IIR, Source C #8):

```
tf-idf(word, chunk) = f × ln(N / n)          N = number of chunks; the score of a chunk = the sum over the question's words
```

We score a toy question, "bpa ban", on six documents of equal length. Document x is a sentence about the BPA ban: it contains each word once. Document y repeats "BPA" six times and never says "ban".

**The result** (toy):

```
                     x: "bpa" once, "ban" once     y: "bpa" six times
textbook TF-IDF              2.8904                     6.5917     ← y wins
BM25 (rank_bm25)             1.8871                     1.1756     ← x wins

one word, more and more repeats (toy, "bpa", average length):
repeats f          1       2       4       8       16
TF-IDF          1.099   2.197   4.394   8.789   17.578     ← grows in a straight line
BM25            0.588   0.840   1.069   1.237    1.344     ← flattens
```

**What this means.** TF-IDF counts every repeat at full value, so a chunk that repeats one word beats a chunk that matches more of your question. Repetition is weak evidence after the first few mentions. BM25 fixes this by letting each extra repeat add less (§5).

**For your project:** there are many TF-IDF variants. scikit-learn's default, for example, smooths its idf, adds 1 to it and normalises each vector, so always say which one you mean. This course uses BM25.

## 4. BM25 exactly as `rank_bm25` 0.2.2 computes it

The book describes BM25 only in words (Ch12 p.5/14). This is the formula the library really runs (read from its installed source):

```
idf(t)     = ln( (N − n + 0.5) / (n + 0.5) )
if idf(t) < 0:  idf(t) = 0.25 × (mean idf of all words, before flooring)     the "floor"
K          = k1 × (1 − b + b × dl / avgdl)                      dl = chunk length in tokens; avgdl = the average
score      = Σ over the question's words of  idf(t) × f × (k1 + 1) / (f + K)       k1 = 1.5, b = 0.75 by default
```

Call the last fraction the **tf part**. It is the term-frequency factor after saturation and length adjustment.

**The setup.** First by hand, on the toy: the word `bpa` in d0. Then the real question.

**The result** (toy, then demonstration on the 91 windows):

```
toy, bpa in d0:   N 6, n 2, f 2, dl 11, avgdl 10.0
                  idf = ln(4.5 / 2.5) = 0.587787     K = 1.5 × (0.25 + 0.75 × 1.1) = 1.6125
                  tf part = 2 × 2.5 / (2 + 1.6125) = 1.384083     score = 0.813546   (library: 0.813546)

"What does BECCS stand for?", 80 of 91 windows score above 0:
rank 1  window 83   beccs 3.577948 × 1.020073 = 3.649768   +   for (×3) 0.838237 × 1.685088 = 1.412504   = 5.0623
rank 2  window 82   beccs 3.577948 × 1.023697 = 3.662734   +   for (×2) 0.838237 × 1.452589 = 1.217614   = 4.8803
rank 3  window 16   no beccs                                    for (×6) 0.838237 × 1.994625 = 1.671968   = 1.6720
```

**What this means.** The rare word carries the score: `beccs` alone gives about 3.65 to each window that holds it. Three details follow from the formula.
- **A very common word gets the floor, not zero.** `for` is in more than half of the windows, so its raw idf is negative (−1.946). The library replaces it with the floor, 0.838 here. That floor decides which BECCS window comes first: window 83 says "for" three times, window 82 twice.
- **Exactly half gives zero.** In the toy, `plastics` is in 3 of 6 documents: ln(3.5 / 3.5) = 0, and 0 is not negative, so it stays 0.
- **A word the index has never seen adds 0.** No error, no warning.

**The tempting wrong idea:** "common words score nothing." In `rank_bm25`, `climate` (77 of 91 windows) gets the floor, 0.838, which is **more** than `emissions` (34 of 91), at 0.511.

**Try it** (the hand computation, and the tokenizer of §6):

```python
import math, re
def tok_norm(text):                                         # lower-case; keep 1.5 and 650,000 whole
    return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+(?:[.,][0-9]+)*", text.lower())
s = "Panel on Climate Change (IPCC)"
print(s.split()[-1], tok_norm(s)[-1])                       # (IPCC) ipcc
N, n, f, dl, avgdl, k1, b = 6, 2, 2, 11, 10.0, 1.5, 0.75    # 'bpa' in toy d0
K = k1 * (1 - b + b * dl / avgdl)
print(round(math.log((N - n + 0.5) / (n + 0.5)) * f * (k1 + 1) / (f + K), 6))   # 0.813546
```

**For your project:** use the formula of the library you run. Other libraries (Lucene, for example) use an idf that never goes negative; that is not checked here (UNVERIFIED).

## 5. Repeats flatten out (k1), and length cuts both ways (b)

**The setup.** Hold the idf fixed and look only at the tf part. First vary the number of mentions f in a chunk of average length. Then vary b for one mention in chunks of different lengths.

**The result** (formula values, k1 = 1.5):

```
mentions f        1      2      3      5      10     100    1000    → limit k1 + 1 = 2.5
tf part         1.000  1.429  1.667  1.923  2.174  2.463  2.496

one mention         chunk half as long    average length    twice as long
b = 0                    1.000                1.000              1.000      ← length ignored
b = 0.375                1.127                1.000              0.816
b = 0.75 (default)       1.290                1.000              0.690
b = 1                    1.429                1.000              0.625      ← full normalisation
```

Read the first row from left to right: ten mentions give 2.174, not 10. No word can ever add more than idf × 2.5. Window 16's six "for"s reach 1.995 of that 2.5. Now read the b table by column. Length normalisation **raises** a chunk shorter than average as well as lowering a long one.

**What this means.**
- **k1 sets how fast repeats stop counting.** A larger k1 lets the second and third mention count for more before the curve flattens.
- **b sets how much length matters.** At b = 0, one mention scores the same in a 5-token and a 20-token chunk (0.588 each, in a small made-up corpus). At the default, the short one wins, 0.829 against 0.496.

**The tempting wrong ideas:** "ten mentions, ten times the score", and "length normalisation only punishes long chunks".

**For your project:** keep the defaults, k1 = 1.5 and b = 0.75; changing them is an experiment, not a guess. Never set k1 = 0 in `rank_bm25`: every chunk without the word then scores NaN (0 / 0).

## 6. Tokenization decides what can match

**The setup.** BM25 compares token strings exactly, so the tokenizer decides whether a match is even possible. The repo's `fusion_retrieval.ipynb` uses `split()` (cut at spaces only) for the chunks (cell 14) and for the question (cell 17). The course tokenizer `tok_norm` lower-cases and keeps only runs of letters and digits, but keeps `1.5` and `650,000` whole.

**The result** (demonstration, 91 windows):

```
                                split()                              tok_norm
BECCS question, tokens          What does BECCS stand for?           what does beccs stand for
the text's token                (BECCS),                             beccs
windows that score above 0      0 of 91                              80 of 91, the BECCS windows first
"IPCC": text token / result     (IPCC)  / 0 windows                  ipcc / rank 1 (score 3.8928)
"emissions" spelled as          emissions 22, emissions. 20,         emissions
                                emissions, 7, Emissions 1
distinct tokens in the PDF      2,630                                1,769
```

When every score is 0, the repo's helper `bm25_retrieval` (`helper_functions.py` L268) still returns five windows, because `np.argsort(scores)[::-1][:5]` picks by position: the last five of the document, [90, 89, 88, 87, 86]. Nothing errors.

**What this means.** Under `split()`, even the question's `for?` keeps its question mark, so not a single word matches. The formula never had a chance. Each tokenizer choice fixes something and can break something else:
- **case:** lower-casing merges `Emissions` and `emissions`;
- **punctuation:** stripping it frees `(IPCC)`;
- **numbers:** a plain `\w+` rule cuts `1.5` into `1` and `5`; `tok_norm` keeps it whole;
- **stemming:** a toy plural rule matches `emission` to `emissions` but also turns `beccs` into `becc`.

**The tempting wrong ideas:**
- "BM25 finds an exact term whenever the text contains it": only when both sides produce the same token.
- "No match means no results": the repo's code returns five arbitrary chunks.
- "BM25 uses the model's tokens": it uses whatever *your* tokenizer outputs, here whole words. The LLM's and the embedder's subword tokens are a different thing (P1).

**For your project:** use one tokenizer for chunks and question. Test it on your own identifiers before indexing, and return only chunks that scored above 0.

## 7. When not to use BM25 alone

| Situation | What we saw | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| The question uses other words than the text | Draft seed D-006 (97 chunks, R1): the evidence talks about solar farms on water and never says "lakes" or "panels". BM25 ranks a page-22 chunk that contains "panels" first and the evidence second; dense ranks the evidence first | Combine BM25 with dense retrieval (R1) |
| The question has no rare word | "What can be done about this?" (91 windows): 81 windows score above 0, and the top score, 5.76, is **higher** than the true BECCS match (5.06) | Never read a raw BM25 score as "a good answer exists" |
| Your identifiers do not fit the tokenizer | `v2.10.3` and `ERR_CONN_RESET`: each rule in this course cuts one of them apart (P3-T-01) | Write and test a tokenizer for your identifiers |
| A tiny or near-duplicate collection | Two documents: no score can be above 0 (a word they share even scores −0.080). Near-duplicates: negative scores (toy) | Test with 3 or more varied documents |

The book's rule of thumb (Ch12 p.12/14): BM25 alone is enough only when every query is a keyword lookup, and vector search alone when every query is a natural-language question.

**Caveat, stated plainly:** on the 8 answerable draft seeds (97 chunks, R1), BM25 alone already had the evidence in the top 4 for all 8, and its first relevant rank was better than dense's (MRR@10 0.938 against 0.708). Those are 8 questions drafted by an LLM, not real users, so this is an observation, not a result.

**For your project:** that is why v2.0 keeps **BM25 alone** as one of its three arms, next to dense and hybrid, and reports every category separately.
