RAG study kit · Phase 4 · concept I4

Proposition chunking: one fact per vector

Why a chunk with many facts can hide the one you ask about, how an LLM rewrites a page into single facts that point back to their sentences, how to check that rewrite, how to score it fairly, and what it costs.

measured local code on the climate PDF and the repo notebook (no API call) · hand-written propositions written by the tutor, not by an LLM · toy made-up data that shows a mechanism · assumed a planning number, not measured · formula computed on this page · external from a cited study

What exists so far. No LLM has written propositions for the course corpus yet: that needs recorded Groq calls (the course's study/llmops/ cache, not built). So this lesson teaches the mechanism and the measurement plan with 40 propositions the tutor wrote by hand from the PDF text, and six planted mistakes. Every ranked list below is a made-up order. The book pages (Ch4) are cited from the course's page notes; they were not re-opened in the session that wrote this page.

THE ANSWER FIRST

Proposition chunking in five sentences

A chunk that holds many facts gets one vector that is a blend of all of them, so a question about one fact can rank that chunk low. Proposition chunking asks an LLM to rewrite each passage as short statements that stand alone, one fact each, with pronouns replaced by names, and indexes those statements instead of the chunk. A rewrite is not text from the document, so every proposition must keep a pointer to the sentences it came from (source_ids): that pointer is what lets code check the rewrite, score it against span gold, and return the original chunk to the generator. It costs LLM calls at index time: one per page to write the propositions, plus one per page if you grade them in a batch, or one per proposition if you grade them one by one (66 against 1,433 calls on the climate PDF). Measure it as "does ranking by propositions bring the right chunk up earlier?", check by hand that the propositions say only what the source says, and expect no gain on contrast and multi-hop questions, where single facts lose the relation between them.

Next comes the whole theory in one read, then a formula sheet, then the step-by-step part. It follows one question from the project's draft seeds, D-002 "In what year was the Paris Agreement adopted?", through page 10 of the climate PDF, and uses D-005 (coal against natural gas) and D-007 (a two-page question) as the hard cases. Short gives a complete first understanding. Full adds the research behind it, the repo notebook read critically, and the book's pages; its parts have a dashed border.

THE THEORY IN ONE READ

The whole topic, idea by idea

Twelve ideas, in the order they build on each other. This part is theory only, with no measurements. Each idea links to its step below.

  1. Dilution: one vector for many facts

    A chunk gets one vector. If it states several unrelated facts, the vector sits between them, so its similarity to a question about one fact is lower than a vector for that fact alone. One unit per fact gives a sharper match.

    toy: facts f₁…fₙ orthonormal, chunk = normalise(Σ fᵢ) → cos(f₁, chunk) = 1 / √n
    Step 1 →

  2. A proposition

    A proposition states one fact, can be understood alone, uses full names instead of pronouns, keeps the dates and qualifiers that make the fact precise, and has one subject with one thing said about it. Dense X Retrieval's short form: distinct, minimal, self-contained.

    Step 2 →

  3. Decontextualisation

    "It provides a framework…" means nothing alone. The rewrite replaces "it" with the name the page used earlier ("The UNFCCC provides…"). The name must come from the page itself, never from the model's own knowledge.

    Step 3 →

  4. Number the units first

    Before any LLM call, code splits each page into numbered units (headings and sentences) and records where each one starts and ends. The model then refers to units by id instead of copying text.

    unit u = (id "page:i", kind, [a, b))  doc_text[a:b] == text
    Step 4 →

  5. Structured generation

    The model returns JSON in a fixed shape: a list of {text, source_ids}. Code validates the shape, logs every failure (bad JSON, a reply cut off by the token limit), and never turns a failure into an empty list without saying so.

    Step 5 →

  6. Every proposition points back

    A proposition's evidence is the union of its source units. Its parent is the chunk that contains that evidence. The proposition's text is what gets embedded; the span is what gets scored and returned.

    span(p) = [ min aᵢ , max bᵢ ) over i ∈ source_ids  ·  parent(p) = the chunk with span(p) ⊆ [start, end)
    Step 6 →

  7. Checking the rewrite

    Code can catch added words: every content word of the proposition should appear in its sources (or in a name taken from earlier on the page). It cannot catch a dropped qualifier or a wrong referent. A person checks a sample.

    align(p) = | W(p) ∩ (W(sources) ∪ W(names)) | / | W(p) |  W = content-word stems
    Step 7 →

  8. The quality grade is a filter

    An LLM grade (keep a proposition if all four 1–10 grades are at least 7) decides what enters the index. It is part of the system, not a measurement of faithfulness, and its threshold is a guess until you check it against your own labels.

    keep-good = kept ∧ good / good  ·  catch-bad = dropped ∧ bad / bad
    Step 8 →

  9. Three ways to score

    (a) Rank propositions, return their parent chunks without duplicates, and score the chunks as usual: the fair comparison with the chunk index. (b) Score the propositions' own spans: a diagnostic that over-credits a lossy rewrite unless you also check the text. (c) Page-sized parents: rejected, because almost any page overlaps the gold.

    arm (a): MRR@10, CE@k on parents after dedup  ·  arm (b): rel(p) = 1 ⇔ span(p) overlaps gold
    Step 9 →

  10. What atomic facts lose

    A contrast ("coal is the most, gas the least carbon-intensive") or a two-step question needs two facts together. Single-fact units can fill the top k with one side. Near-duplicates crowd the list too. And no proposition can retrieve a sentence that never got one.

    distinct-facts@k · sentence_coverage · gold_reachability
    Step 10 →

  11. Cost

    Index-time calls grow with pages (writing) and, if you grade one by one, with propositions. The index grows by the number of propositions per chunk. The daily token quota, not money, is the limit.

    calls = pages + pages (batched grade) or + propositions (one by one)  ·  size = n × dim × 4 bytes
    Step 11 →

  12. A fair test in the project

    Index the whole corpus, compare against the current best chunk index at the same k and the same token budget, check faithfulness by hand with an interval, guard the contrast and multi-hop questions, and expect "inconclusive" on a small test set.

    Step 12 →

STEP 1 · THE FAILURE IT FIXES

One vector for many facts points at none of them

D-002 asks "In what year was the Paris Agreement adopted?" The answer, 2015, is in one sentence on page 10. In v0's index that sentence sits in chunk 27, the first 951 characters of page 10. The same chunk also says what the UNFCCC is, that it frames the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement, that cooperation under it matters, the 2 °C and 1.5 °C goals, that countries submit NDCs, and how carbon pricing works. In the tutor's hand-written set, 14 propositions come from chunk 27. The chunk gets one vector for all of them.

A toy shows why that hurts. Give each fact its own direction, at right angles to the others, and let the chunk's vector be the normalised sum of its n facts. A question that asks about exactly one fact then has cosine 1/√n with the chunk. With one fact it is 1.000; with 3 it is 0.577; with 14 it is 0.267. A short distractor that is only fairly close to the question (cosine 0.60) beats the right chunk as soon as the chunk holds 3 facts.

So the fix is to give each fact its own vector. That is the retrieval half of the idea; the other half is that the generator still needs the surrounding text, which is why the propositions point back to their chunk ("retrieve small, generate big", step 9).

Common mistake: "bigger chunks give more context, so they also retrieve better." Retrieval and generation want different sizes: a small unit matches a question more sharply, a larger one gives the answer its context. Proposition indexing uses the small unit to find and the chunk to answer.

In your project: this is I1's chunk-size question pushed to its limit. I1 changes the size of the chunk; I4 changes what gets a vector.

STEP 2 · THE UNIT

What counts as a proposition

The repo's notebook gives the model five rules (proposition_chunking.ipynb cell 12), which match the book's description (Ch4 p.5–6/15). In plain words:

RuleWhat it meansFails if…
1 · One factit states one specific fact or claimit joins two facts with "and"
2 · Stands alonea reader understands it with no surrounding textit needs the paragraph to make sense
3 · Full namesno pronouns or vague referencesit starts "It…" or "These policies…"
4 · Dates and qualifiersit keeps what makes the fact precise"well below 2 °C" becomes "below 2 °C"
5 · One subject–predicateone thing, and one thing said about itit has several clauses

The research paper behind the idea, Dense X Retrieval (Chen et al., 2023, external), puts it in three words: distinct (one meaning), minimal (cannot be split further) and self-contained (all the context it needs is inside it).

Sentence 10:7 holds three facts. Written as propositions hand-written:

10:7 The Paris Agreement, adopted in 2015, aims to limit global warming to well below 2 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels, with efforts to limit the increase to 1.5 degrees Celsius.

  • p10-07 The Paris Agreement was adopted in 2015.
  • p10-08 The Paris Agreement aims to limit global warming to well below 2 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels.
  • p10-09 Efforts under the Paris Agreement aim to limit the increase in global warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius.

Each has one fact, a full name ("The Paris Agreement", never "it"), its qualifiers ("well below", "above pre-industrial levels"), and the page's own words. Only p10-07 answers D-002.

Across the whole sample the tutor wrote 40 propositions from 26 sentences: 1.54 per sentence. Dense X reports 257 million propositions from 114 million Wikipedia sentences, 2.25 per sentence (README, external). An LLM usually splits finer than a careful person does; step 11 plans with 2.25.

Common mistake: "a proposition is a short sentence cut out of the text." It is a rewrite. One sentence can give several propositions, and each must make sense alone, which a fragment cut from the middle of a sentence does not.

In your project: keep the page's own wording where you can. It makes the code checks in step 7 work, and it makes the rewrite easy to compare with its source.

STEP 3 · STANDING ALONE

Replace "it" with the name the page used

A sentence that starts with a pronoun leans on the sentence before it. Its proposition must name the thing. Decontextualisation means rewriting a statement so that it carries its own context. Three real cases from the PDF:

Source unitProposition hand-writtenName taken from
10:4 "It provides a framework for negotiating specific protocols and agreements, such as the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement."The UNFCCC provides a framework for negotiating the Paris Agreement. (and one each for the Kyoto Protocol and for "specific protocols and agreements")the heading 10:2 and sentence 10:3
29:14 "These meetings play a crucial role in advancing global climate action."COP meetings play a crucial role in advancing global climate action.29:12 and 29:13
2:2 "It is mined extensively in countries like China, India, and the United States…"Coal is mined extensively in countries like China, India, and the United States.2:0 and 2:1

The first case matters for a real question. D-007 asks for "the supreme decision-making body of the convention that provides the framework for negotiating the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement". The fact that links the question to the UNFCCC is in sentence 10:4, which never names the UNFCCC. Only after the rewrite does a unit say "The UNFCCC provides a framework for negotiating the Paris Agreement."

The name must come from the page, not from the model's memory. So each proposition records the names it brought in (resolved), and code checks that each one occurs earlier on the same page than the proposition's first source unit. In the tutor's set, 15 of the 40 propositions use a resolved name, and all 15 pass. A wrong name that does occur on the page (the Paris Agreement instead of the UNFCCC for 10:4) passes this check; only a person catches it (step 7).

Common mistake: "decontextualising means adding background so the fact is clearer." Adding background is adding facts. The rewrite only swaps a reference for the name the page already used.

In your project: I5 (contextual chunk headers) solves the same problem for a whole chunk by putting the heading path in front of it. I4 does it inside every unit. Both are compared in I-CMP.

STEP 4 · BEFORE ANY LLM CALL

Number the sentences first

Code splits each page into units: heading lines and sentences, each with an id page:i and its character offsets. A heading is a short line that starts with a capital letter, has no end punctuation and is not inside an unfinished sentence. A sentence ends at ., ! or ? followed by a space or a line end. Page 10 becomes 27 units:

  • Whole PDF: 621 sentences and 286 heading lines, 907 units on 33 pages; 6 to 22 sentences per page, median 19. Every unit passes doc_text[a:b] == text.
  • Tests that pass: "1.5 degrees" stays in one sentence (the full stop is followed by a digit, not a space); "pre-industrial" and the line break inside 10:7 stay put; "Paris Agreement" (10:6) is its own heading unit; a page that starts in lower case (29:0, the end of a p.28 sentence) is not a heading.
  • Known limits: a sentence that crosses a page is cut in two. 8 pages end with a cut sentence, and 8 pages start with the rest of one: unit 23:0 is just resources., the end of a sentence that began on page 22. "e.g." would also split a sentence (it never occurs in this PDF).

Why bother? The model can now say "this proposition comes from [10:7]" instead of copying text, and code can check the claim. Offsets turn ids into spans (step 6).

Common mistake: "split the text on every full stop." That breaks "1.5 degrees" into two sentences and glues headings onto the next sentence.

In your project: the splitter is deterministic code with tests, versioned like the chunker. Its limits go in the run notes, because a split error becomes a wrong source id.

STEP 5 · GENERATION

A frozen prompt and a fixed output shape

The course's prompt, prop_gen_v1, was written for I4. It gives the five rules, asks for the page's own wording, forbids outside knowledge, and asks for source_ids. Its one example is about a made-up town library, so it shares nothing with the corpus or the test questions:

Example input:
[0:0] (heading) Town library
[0:1] The town library opened in 1998.
[0:2] It lends books and audio recordings.
[0:3] It also runs a weekly reading club for children.

Example output:
{"propositions": [
 {"text": "The town library opened in 1998.", "source_ids": ["0:1"]},
 {"text": "The town library lends books.", "source_ids": ["0:2"]},
 {"text": "The town library lends audio recordings.", "source_ids": ["0:2"]},
 {"text": "The town library runs a weekly reading club for children.", "source_ids": ["0:3"]}
]}

Compare the repo's example (cell 12). Its sentence says that Neil Armstrong first walked on the Moon in 1969 during Apollo 11; its first proposition is "Neil Armstrong was an astronaut." The sentence never says that. The example teaches the model to add what it knows, and the repo's grader (cell 15) gives that proposition 10 out of 10 for accuracy. Our example adds nothing.

The page goes in as numbered units. For page 10 the rendered prompt is 3,858 characters, about 682 tokens derived from P1's tokens-per-character. The reply must match a JSON schema: an object with a list propositions, each item with a string text and a list of strings source_ids, both required, nothing else allowed.

  • Ask for strict JSON where the provider supports it (Groq's json_schema with strict: true on gpt-oss-20b: search-checked, not confirmed), and validate in code anyway.
  • Log two different failures: a reply that is not valid JSON, and a reply cut off because it hit the token limit (finish_reason = "length"). gpt-oss-20b writes reasoning tokens first and they count as output, so a small limit can leave an empty or half-written list (P1).
  • Never turn a failure into silence. LangChain's propositional-retrieval template catches any error and returns an empty list, with no log (research, verified). The page then drops out of the index and nothing tells you.
  • Read the model id from the config and record the id the provider returns. The repo's llama-3.1-70b-versatile is retired.
Full · two framework defaults

LangChain's with_structured_output works by forcing a tool call (tool_choice="any", langchain-core 1.6.6, verified by the research). Forced tool use is refused by Claude Opus 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5, so the course's adapter overrides it to use the provider's own JSON-schema output. Claude's structured output does not enforce minimum or maximum in a schema, so 1–10 grades need a code check.

Common mistake: "the reply passed the schema, so it is right." A schema checks the shape. A proposition can be perfectly shaped and say something the page never said.

In your project: the prompt and schema files are frozen and hashed, and the cache key includes them, so a changed prompt is a new run, never a silent edit.

STEP 6 · THE POINTER

Every proposition points back to its source

A proposition's span runs from the start of its first source unit to the end of its last (all on one page). Its parent is the v0 chunk that contains that span. Click a proposition to see its sources:

  • All 40 of the tutor's propositions have source ids that exist and spans inside the document.
  • 34 have one parent, 6 have two: their sentence sits in the 200-character overlap between two chunks. The rule picks the lower chunk number as the primary parent.

This is why a proposition is not a chunk. The course's chunk contract (06 §2.3) says doc_text[char_start:char_end] == text. A proposition's text is a rewrite, so the contract fails. The project needs an IndexUnit: the text that gets embedded (the proposition) kept apart from the evidence that gets scored and returned (the span, the source ids, the parent chunk ids), plus the prompt version and model that wrote it.

Common mistake: "store each proposition as a chunk with its own text, and score it like any chunk." Then span gold cannot be applied, and the generator receives rewritten text instead of the document's own words.

In your project: v1.4 adds IndexUnit {unit_id, embed_text, doc_id, char_start, char_end, source_ids, parent_chunk_ids, prompt_version, model}. The scorer reads only the span fields.

STEP 7 · TRUST, BUT CHECK

Checking the rewrite: what code catches and what it cannot

Alignment asks: does every content word of the proposition appear in its sources? Content words are the words left after removing short function words ("the", "is", "of"…), cut to a rough stem ("negotiating" and "negotiate" both become negotiat). Names the proposition took from earlier on the page count as supported.

align(p) = | W(p) ∩ (W(sources) ∪ W(resolved names)) | / | W(p) |

Six planted mistakes show what this catches measured:

Planted mistakealignCaught by
added fact: "…was signed in Paris by 196 countries."0.400align (any rule)
wrong year: "…was adopted in 2016."0.750align < 1 only
repo example: "Neil Armstrong was an astronaut."0.667align < 1 only
dropped qualifier: "…to below 2 degrees Celsius." (was "well below")1.000nothing in code
wrong referent: "The Paris Agreement provides a framework…" (10:4 means the UNFCCC)1.000nothing in code
pronoun start: "It provides a framework…"1.000the pronoun check
bad id: source 10:31 (page 10 has units 0 to 26)—the id check

Two lessons. First, a cut-off such as "flag align below 0.5" misses the astronaut and the wrong year, so list every proposition with any unsupported word (align below 1) and read the list. Second, alignment sees only added words. A dropped qualifier or a wrong referent uses only words from the source, so no word check can see it. All 40 hand-written propositions score 1.000, which shows the check runs, not that they are right.

That is why a person labels a sample: each proposition is supported, added (says more than the source) or distorted (says something different). Report the share supported with a Wilson interval (P6), because a small sample is uncertain:

Common mistake: "align is 1, so the proposition is faithful." Align 1 means no new words. It says nothing about words that went missing or a name swapped for another one from the same page.

In your project: the code checks run on every proposition; a hand sample of at least 30 (better 60) is labelled with a Wilson lower bound. A proposition a person finds wrong becomes a test case that the checks must flag in future.

STEP 8 · THE QUALITY GATE

The 7/10 grade is a filter, and a costly one

The repo grades every proposition with a second LLM call on four scales from 1 to 10 (accuracy, clarity, completeness, conciseness) and keeps it only if all four reach 7 (cells 15–16; the book describes the same gate, Ch4 p.7–9/15). On its essay the saved output drops 2 of 31 propositions (0.065): one for completeness 6, one for conciseness 6. 29 are embedded.

Read the grader before trusting it measured · cells 15–16:

  • All 5 of its examples are graded 10/10/10/10, so it has never seen a failing case. The astronaut proposition is one of them.
  • The same model writes and grades the propositions.
  • The message it grades is "{proposition}, {original_text}", with no labels saying which part is which; the example labels have typos ("Propositons" five times, "Evaluation_3" twice).
  • The threshold 7 was never checked against anything (misconception MC-006: an uncalibrated LLM score treated as truth).

So the grade is part of the system: it decides what enters the index. It is not a measurement of faithfulness. To know whether it helps, compare its keep/drop decisions with your own labels from step 7:

keep-good = kept and good / all good  (does it keep what is right?)
catch-bad = dropped and bad / all bad  (does it remove what is wrong?)

Report both with their n. A filter that drops 6% at random would also "drop 2 of 31".

It also costs the most. Grading one proposition per call means about 1,400 calls on the climate PDF; grading all of a page's propositions in one call means 33. Run the cheap code checks of step 7 first and grade only what passes.

Common mistake (MC-006): "the propositions scored at least 7 out of 10, so they are faithful." The score comes from a model that writes and grades with the same blind spots, against a threshold nobody checked.

In your project: batched grading per page is the default; one-by-one grading is a measured ablation on one page. Grader on against grader off is an experiment arm, judged by retrieval metrics and your labels, never by the grader's own scores (D-10).

STEP 9 · MEASURING IT

Three ways to score propositions, and only one is a fair A/B

The course's gold is a character span (06 §6.7). A chunk is relevant when it overlaps the span; complete evidence (CE@k) needs every gold group covered by what is returned (P6). A proposition's text is not in the document, so you score it through its pointer, in one of three ways:

ScoringHowWhat it measures
(a) Parents · primaryrank propositions by similarity; replace each by its parent chunk; keep the first time each parent appears; score the chunks with the usual ruleswhether proposition vectors rank the same chunks better. The generator gets chunks either way, so this is the fair comparison with the chunk index
(b) Spans · diagnostica proposition counts if its span overlaps the gold; CE@k over the union of the top-k spanswhether the retrieved units come from the evidence. It over-credits a rewrite that lost the fact, so pair it with a text check: count p only if its text states the fact
(c) Page parents · rejectedthe page a proposition came fromalmost any page overlaps the gold, so Hit and MRR only measure which page was found

Work it on a made-up ranked list for D-002 made-up order, not model output. Choose a list:

For D-002: arm (a) returns chunk 27 first, so RR = 1.000. Arm (b) also says 1.000, because p10-08 shares sentence 10:7 with the gold. But p10-08 talks about the 2 °C goal and never says "2015". The text check finds the first proposition that states the year at rank 3: RR = 0.333. Arm (b) alone would have credited a unit that cannot answer the question.

Look at the parents too. The top 4 propositions give only 2 distinct parents (27 and 68). LangChain's MultiVectorRetriever does the same: it asks for k child hits and keeps the first copy of each parent, so k children give at most k parents, often fewer (research, verified). Its k is the vector store's own (its search_kwargs default to {}, and most stores then return 4), and it counts children, not parents. The repo's own runs show it: in cells 20 and 32, all four proposition hits come from chunk 3, so a parent return would give one chunk, not four.

Common mistake: "both arms used k = 4, so the comparison is fair." Four propositions can return one or two chunks. Say whether k counts children or parents; to compare with a chunk index, read deeper until you have k parents, or compare at the same token budget.

In your project: arm (a) is the v1.4 headline metric (MRR@10 and CE@4 on parents, the same scorer as v0). Arm (b) and the text check are logged diagnostics.

STEP 10 · THE BOUNDARY

What atomic facts lose

Splitting into single facts throws away the links between them. Three effects, each visible in the lists above:

  • Contrast. D-005 asks how the document contrasts coal and natural gas. Put the four coal propositions first: arm (b) has CE@4 = 0, because no natural-gas proposition is in the top 4. Arm (a) returns chunk 3, which holds both sentences, so CE@4 = 1. The parent brings the relation back.
  • Multi-hop. D-007 needs two facts on two pages. With the "UNFCCC frames the Paris Agreement" proposition (p10-05) at rank 4, arm (b) has CE@4 = 1. Move it to rank 5 and CE@4 = 0: the gold span on page 10 includes sentence 10:4, and no other proposition in the top 4 points to it. Arm (a) stays at 1, because chunk 27 holds the whole span.
  • Near-duplicates. Pages 10 and 23 repeat the Paris paragraph, so two pairs of propositions have the same text (p10-08 and p23-02, p10-09 and p23-03): 38 distinct texts out of 40. In the D-002 list they fill two of the top 4: distinct-facts@4 = 3. The repo shows the same in cells 28 and 36: 3 of the top 4 hits say "Founder Mode is…". Parent dedup hides this in arm (a); distinct-facts@k shows it.

Two ceilings limit arm (b) before any ranking happens:

sentence_coverage = sentences with ≥ 1 proposition / sentences
gold_reachability = gold alternatives overlapped by some span(p) / gold alternatives

The tutor's sample covers page 10 fully (16/16) but only 4 of 6 sentences in the coal and gas paragraphs of page 2: the methane sentence and the fracking sentence were left out on purpose, and no proposition can ever retrieve them. Its gold_reachability over the 8 answerable seeds is 6/12 = 0.500, simply because it covers only parts of four pages; a full run must cover every page.

Common mistake: "the more atomic the units, the better retrieval gets." Atomic units help single-fact lookups and hurt questions that need facts together. They also multiply near-duplicates.

In your project: D-005 and D-007 are the guard questions for v1.4. Report CE@4 for both arms, distinct-facts@4, sentence_coverage and the uncovered sentences.

STEP 11 · THE PRICE

What it costs to build and keep

The counts come from the measured 621 sentences; the tokens per call are assumed planning numbers from the research (writing: 1,050 in and 1,600 out per page, reasoning included; batched grade: 1,800 and 1,600 per page; one-by-one grade: 870 and 300 per proposition). Prices and limits: Groq gpt-oss-20b, $0.075 / $0.30 per million tokens, 200K tokens a day, 8K a minute (the plan row is not confirmed).

Money is not the obstacle here; the daily quota is. One batched build of the whole PDF uses about a full day of the free tier, and one-by-one grading more than a week.

Common mistake: "it is one LLM call per chunk, so it is cheap." The one-by-one quality grade is one call per proposition, about 14 for every chunk here, and it dominates the bill.

In your project: the tool forecasts calls, tokens and quota days before any call, and the run refuses to start if it would cross the budget.

STEP 12 · YOUR PROJECT

v1.4: how to test it fairly, and when not to

Index the whole corpus, not 5 pages. The plan's wording (06 v1.4, "a subset, for example 5 pages") invites a trap. Five pages hold about 15 chunks, so k = 4 returns a quarter of the index, and almost anything scores well. The repo's comparison is the extreme case: its chunk index has 3 chunks and k = 4, so every query returns the whole index and nothing is ranked. A full build is affordable (66 calls, about one day of quota). If you need fewer questions, restrict the questions, never the index.

  1. The comparison: arm (a) against the current best chunk index (and v0), on the same questions, at the same k and at the same token budget (choose k so the mean context tokens match).
  2. The diagnostics: arm (b) with the text check; distinct-facts@4; sentence_coverage and gold_reachability; parse-failure and cut-off rates.
  3. The guards: CE@4 on D-005 and D-007; the hand faithfulness sample (at least 30 units, Wilson lower bound reported).
  4. The leakage test: no 8 consecutive words shared between the prompt (with its example) and any eval question or gold quote. The clean prompt shares 0; replacing the example sentence with D-002's gold quote gives 10 shared 8-word runs, so the test fails as it should. Book text never goes into a prompt.
  5. The verdict: pre-register "inconclusive" as a likely result. Dev holds about 12–16 answerable questions; the smallest CE@4 difference it can detect is about 0.4 (Phase 4 research).

Where it sits among chunking strategies. Propositions are one option in a family: fixed-size and recursive splitting (C2, I1), structure-aware chunks that follow headings (I2), semantic chunks cut where the topic shifts (I3), contextual headers (I5) and late chunking. Each trades cost against how sharply a unit matches a question. You pick one the same way every time: measure on your own labelled questions, at the same k and the same token budget (I-CMP compares them all).

When not to use it

  • Most questions compare or summarise (they need facts together, step 10).
  • The documents change often: every change pays the build again.
  • The embedder is already strong and trained on passages: Dense X's gains were small for such retrievers, and a 2026 reproduction found propositions worse than plain splitting (step 14, both external and not confirmed here).
  • The hand check finds the rewrite unfaithful (Wilson lower bound under 0.85, or many unsupported words).
  • A cheaper fix works: contextual headers (I5) also fix "it" and "these meetings", at one call per chunk or none.

In your project: v1.4 is adopted only if arm (a) improves on dev, holds on the one logged test run, keeps D-005 and D-007, and passes the faithfulness bound. Otherwise the result is recorded and the chunk index stays.

STEP 14 · WHAT THE RESEARCH SAYS

The evidence for and against

  • Dense X Retrieval (Chen et al., 2023). English Wikipedia indexed as 41M passages, 114M sentences or 257M propositions (README, verified). Reported: Recall@20 up about 10 points on average for unsupervised retrievers and about 2.7 for supervised ones, more for rare entities, and more answers inside a fixed word budget (search-checked; the numbers were not opened). Limits: Wikipedia factoid questions; a propositionizer trained on Wikipedia; small gains for retrievers trained on passages. Their fixed-word-budget comparison is the same idea as the course's equal-token-budget rule.
  • The released propositionizer (chentong00/propositionizer-wiki-flan-t5-large): a no-API option, about 3 GB of weights (not confirmed), slow on a CPU. Its input is "Title / Section / Content" and a parse failure gives an empty list. Not in the core path.
  • LangChain's propositional-retrieval template (tag langchain==0.2.0, verified): a prompt adapted from the paper, a forced tool call, 10 calls at a time, any failure turned into an empty list without a log, and 8,000-character parents. With parents that large, the precision of the small unit is lost again at the context stage.
  • FActScore (verified): splits text one sentence at a time into atomic facts and keeps the sentence → facts mapping. That is the cleanest answer to the span problem, and the reason the course asks for source ids.
  • Against: Zhou, Wang, Koopman and Zuccon (2026) reproduced several chunking methods and report propositions worse than fixed-size, sentence and paragraph splitting for in-corpus retrieval (for example 0.3888 against 0.4849 with Jina-v3), and among the slowest (search-checked, not confirmed). LumberChunker (2024) found propositions weaker on narrative text. Expect the null result.
