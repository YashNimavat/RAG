Retrieval framing: relevance, qrels and the index

What it means for a chunk to be relevant, who decides, how a search engine finds candidates, and how you measure a retriever without fooling yourself.

measured from a 10-document toy corpus written for P2 and the course's climate PDF (local code, no API call) · toy made-up data that shows a mechanism · formula computed on this page · external from a cited study

THE ANSWER FIRST

Retrieval framing in five sentences

A retriever never sees relevance: it computes a similarity score for each pair of question and chunk, sorts the chunks, and cuts the list at k. Relevance is a person's judgment about the need behind the question (does this chunk help answer it?), written down under a guideline and stored as qrels: question, chunk, grade. Similarity is only the system's stand-in for relevance, and it fails in known ways: a chunk that echoes the question, a footer stuffed with keywords, an answer written in other words. Keyword search finds its candidates through an inverted index (word → chunks), so a chunk that shares no word with the question can never be returned. You measure retrieval by comparing a run with the qrels, asking two separate questions (what is in the set, and how high it sits), and the numbers are only as good as the labels, because a chunk nobody judged counts as irrelevant.

Next comes the whole theory in one read, then a formula sheet, then the step-by-step part. It follows the book's own example question, "What is the main cause of climate change?" (Ch1 p.8/14), through a 10-document toy collection whose labels were written by hand, then shows the same ideas on the 91 windows of the climate PDF. Short gives a complete first understanding. Full adds the index algorithms, the five kinds of relevance, label agreement and the checks against the repo and the book; its parts have a dashed border.

THE THEORY IN ONE READ

The whole topic, idea by idea

Twelve ideas, in the order they build on each other. This part is theory only, with no measurements. Each idea links to its step below, where the toy collection and the climate PDF show it working.

  1. Collection, document, need, query

    The collection is everything you can search; each searchable unit is a document (in RAG, a chunk). The information need is what the person wants to know; the query is the words they typed. One query can hide two different needs, so relevance is judged against the need.

    C = { d₁ … d_N }  need ≠ query text
    Step 1 →

  2. A retriever scores, sorts and cuts

    For one query, a retriever computes one score per document, sorts the documents by score with a stated rule for ties, and keeps the first k. The result is a run. Nothing in it says "relevant".

    run_q = sort_desc( s(q, d) for d in C ) → R_k = the first k
    Step 2 →

  3. Similarity is not relevance

    The score measures how alike the query and the chunk look, by shared words or by close vectors. Relevance is whether the chunk helps a person with the need. The score is the system's stand-in for relevance, and it can be fooled.

    s(q, d) is computed  ·  rel(q, d) ∈ {0, 1, 2} is judged
    Step 2 →

  4. Relevance labels: qrels

    A person reads each (query, document) pair under a written guideline and gives a grade: 2 answers it, 1 is part of the answer, 0 does not help. The labels are stored as qrels. Any pair not listed counts as 0, and "relevant" means a grade of at least 1.

    qrels line = (qid, doc, grade)  ·  unlisted ⇒ 0  ·  relevant ⇔ grade ≥ 1
    Step 3 →

  5. "Relevance" means different things

    The same word is used for the retriever's score (system relevance), for being about the topic (topical relevance), and for actually helping answer (situational relevance). Your labels must say which one they measure; in this course it is the third.

    Step 4 →

  6. The inverted index

    Keyword search does not read every document for every query. It keeps, for each word, the sorted list of documents that contain it (its postings). A word's document frequency is simply the length of its list.

    P(t) = sorted { d : t ∈ d }  ·  df(t) = | P(t) |
    Step 5 →

  7. Boolean AND and OR

    The oldest kind of search combines postings lists: AND keeps documents that contain every word (often too few), OR keeps documents that contain any word (often too many). The work done is the number of postings read.

    AND = ∩ P(t)  ·  OR = ∪ P(t)  ·  cost = Σ | P(t) |
    Step 6 →

  8. Ranking from the index, and ties

    Ranked keyword search adds a weight for each matched word, but only documents in at least one postings list can score above 0: those are its candidates. When two documents score the same, a tie rule decides their order, and that rule can change a metric.

    score(d) = Σ over query words t with d ∈ P(t) of w(t, d)  here w = 1
    Step 7 →

  9. Set quality: precision and recall

    Treat the top k as a set. Precision asks how much of what you returned is relevant; recall asks how much of what is relevant you returned. Recall needs to know how many relevant documents exist.

    precision = | R ∩ Rel | / | R |  ·  recall = | R ∩ Rel | / | Rel |
    Step 8 →

  10. Ranking quality

    Two runs can return the same set in a different order. The rank of the first relevant document, and its reciprocal, measure how high the evidence sits. P6 builds the full family of ranking metrics on this.

    r₁ = rank of the first relevant document  ·  RR = 1 / r₁
    Step 9 →

  11. Labels are incomplete and noisy

    Nobody judges every pair. A document nobody judged counts as not relevant, so a retriever that finds unjudged documents is under-scored. Two careful judges also disagree; agreement beyond chance is measured with κ.

    Judged@k = judged in top k / k  ·  Hole@k = 1 − Judged@k  ·  κ = (P(A) − P(E)) / (1 − P(E))
    Step 10 →

  12. A test collection

    Documents, needs and labels together form a test collection. The classic rule of thumb is about 50 needs; you tune on one part (dev) and report on another (test). Labels measure retrieval only: a reference answer is not a relevance label.

    test collection = ( C, needs, qrels )
    Step 12 →

STEP 1 · WHAT THE PERSON WANTS

The words are not the need

The book's first example retrieves the chunks nearest to "What is the main cause of climate change?" and simply calls them relevant (Ch1 p.8/14). This lesson tests that claim instead of assuming it. The vocabulary first:

TermMeaningIn our example
Collectioneverything that can be searched10 short toy documents (later: 91 windows of the climate PDF)
Documentone searchable unit; in RAG, a chunkd01 … d10
Information needwhat the person wants to knowwhat mainly drives climate change
Querythe words they typedq1: main cause of climate change

The need and the query are not the same thing. Type carbon dioxide into the toy collection: one person wants to know what puts it into the air (d01: burning coal, oil and gas), another what takes it out (d06: forests absorb it). Both documents contain the words, but each helps only one of the two needs. The IR textbook's classic example is "python", which can mean the snake or the language (Manning et al., §8.1, external). So relevance is judged against the need, which is why each test question carries a note on what the person wants.

Common mistake: "a chunk is relevant if it contains the query's words." Containing the words is what the retriever checks. Whether it helps with the need is what a person checks.

In your project: every eval question gets a one-line need note, and labels are written against that note, not against the question's wording.

STEP 2 · WHAT THE RETRIEVER DOES

Two retrievers score q1, and both put an echo first

Score q1 against the 10 documents with two very different retrievers. The first counts how many distinct query words a document shares with the query. The second is search by meaning (dense retrieval): an embedding model turns each text into a list of 384 numbers (a vector), and the score is the cosine between the query's vector and the document's (P4 explains vectors properly). Both compute one score for each (query, document) pair; there is no table of every document against every other.

RankKeyword overlapMiniLM cosineWhat the document is
1d02 · 5 wordsd02 · 0.8402d02 repeats the question and answers nothing
2d01 · 3 wordsd01 · 0.6342d01 names the cause: burning fossil fuels
3d08 · 2 wordsd04 · 0.5139d08 is a footer stuffed with "climate change"

The book makes the same point twice: a chunk full of navigation links can score well and help nothing (Ch3 p.1/14), and three chunks about France can sit closest to a question about its capital while none of them names the city (Ch13 p.1–2/13).

Common mistake (MC-002): "the system builds a similarity matrix." Retrieval embeds the question with the same model as the chunks and computes one score per chunk for that question; it never compares all documents with each other.

In your project: a high score is a reason to look, not a label. Every retrieval result is checked against labels written by a person.

STEP 3 · WHO DECIDES RELEVANCE

A person judges each pair, under a written guideline

Relevance comes from a person reading each (query, document) pair and asking: does this help with the need? To keep the answers consistent, the judge follows a written guideline. The toy uses guideline v1:

2 · answers it

The document alone answers the query.

1 · part of it

It gives part of the answer, or a necessary piece.

0 · does not help

Even if it shares the words or the topic.

Read one row. For q2, "why do sea levels rise", d04 gets 2 (warm water expands and glaciers melt: that is the answer), d05 gets 1 (melting ice sheets add water: one reason), and everything else 0, including d10, which is about heat but not about the sea. The ten toy texts are listed in step 6's explorer, so you can try labelling a query yourself.

The labels are stored as qrels (from "query relevance judgments"), one line per judged pair: q1 d01 2. Three rules keep them honest:

  • Any pair not listed counts as 0, and "relevant" means a grade of at least 1.
  • Write a reason for every non-zero label and for every tempting zero: d02 got 0 because "it repeats the question word for word but gives no answer".
  • Judge before you look at any run, so the retriever's ranking cannot steer you; when the guideline changes, it gets a new version number.

Common mistake: "the retriever's top results are the relevant ones." That is circular: the labels exist precisely to check the retriever.

In your project: the course stores labels as evidence spans (06 evidence_groups) with a guideline version, written before any run is inspected (M1).

STEP 4 · ONE WORD, SEVERAL MEANINGS

Three meanings of "relevance"

Books, libraries and papers use "relevance" for different things. Take d02 for q1:

MeaningQuestion it answersd02 for q1
System relevance (the score)How alike do the texts look to the retriever?highest of all
Topical relevanceIs it about the same subject?yes
Situational relevance (usefulness)Does it help this person with this need?no: grade 0

The course's labels measure the third kind. The book uses the word in yet more ways: "context relevance" is an LLM scoring each retrieved chunk at evaluation time (Ch22 p.4/19), and "relevancy grading" is an LLM filter that drops chunks before generation (App p.20/23). A filter is not a label: it is part of the system being tested. And an LLM that writes labels is a judge, so in this course it runs only once, in the end-of-course judge batch, checked against your own labels (D-10).

Full · five manifestations, and LLM assessors

Saracevic's review of relevance research (JASIST 2007, external) lists five manifestations: system (algorithmic), topical (aboutness), cognitive (pertinence to what the person knows), situational (utility for the task) and affective (satisfaction). Evaluation always compares one kind (the system's score) with another (a person's judgment).

Since 2023, LLMs have been used to write qrels. A Bing team reported LLM labels as good as careful human labellers and cheaper, but sensitive to small changes in the prompt (Thomas et al. 2023, abstract, external); UMBRELA is an open reproduction with a 0–3 scale (2024, external). The repo's open-rag-eval-example.ipynb uses that scale. An LLM that writes labels is a judge, so in this course it waits for the end-of-course judge batch and is validated against human labels (D-10).

In your project: write the guideline in terms of usefulness ("helps answer"), and never let a pipeline's own grader produce the labels that measure that pipeline.

STEP 5 · HOW KEYWORD SEARCH FINDS CANDIDATES

The inverted index: word → documents

Picture a table with one row per word and one column per document, a tick where the word occurs (a term–document incidence matrix). Almost every cell is empty, so search engines store only the ticks, row by row: for each word, the sorted list of documents that contain it. That list is the word's postings, and the whole structure is an inverted index, inverted because it turns "document → its words" into "word → its documents".

It is the same idea as the index at the back of a cookbook, which lists for each ingredient the pages that mention it (Ch12 p.3/14): organised by word, not by page. On the toy collection the index has 88 distinct words and 114 postings, and 71 words appear in only one document. Before any of this, a tokenizer cuts each text into words; here it lower-cases and keeps runs of letters and digits, so "Sea-level RISE, 2019!" becomes sea level rise 2019. The whole build fits in a few lines:

def build_index(docs):                       # docs: {doc_id: text}
    index = {}
    for d in sorted(docs):
        for t in sorted(set(re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", docs[d].lower()))):
            index.setdefault(t, []).append(d)   # postings come out sorted by doc id
    return index                             # df(t) = len(index[t])

Common mistake (MC-001 in another form): "the index uses the model's tokens." An index token is whatever your index tokenizer outputs (here, whole words); an LLM's sub-word tokens are a different thing (P1, P3 step 9).

Full · a library that does not invert

The course's BM25 library, rank_bm25 0.2.2, has no inverted index at all: it keeps one word counter per document and scans every document for each query, which is fine at 91 windows.

In your project: an index belongs to one tokenizer and one chunking; change either and every postings list changes.

STEP 6 · THE OLDEST SEARCH

Boolean AND is too strict, OR is too loose

With postings in hand, Boolean search is set arithmetic. AND keeps the documents in every word's list (an intersection); OR keeps those in any list (a union). The work is the number of postings read. Try it on the toy collection:

QueryANDORPostings read
q1 main cause of climate changed02d01, d02, d0810
q2 why do sea levels rise—d042
q3 renewable energy——0
q4 where does methane come from—d06, d092
q5 is extreme heat becoming more common—d02, d03, d105
 measured · toy The explorer recomputes these from the documents.

Why AND is cheap. Postings are kept sorted by document id, so two lists are intersected in one pass, walking both at once: the cost grows with the two lengths added, O(x + y). With several words, intersect the shortest lists first (smallest df first), so the intermediate result stays small (Manning, Raghavan and Schütze, Introduction to Information Retrieval §1.3, external).

The q3 row is vocabulary mismatch: the answer exists, but in other words. No amount of keyword cleverness finds it; that is the job of dense retrieval (P4), or of hybrid search (R1).

Common mistake: "AND is safer because it is precise." It is precise only when it returns anything; for 4 of our 5 queries it returns nothing.

In your project: list the questions whose words never occur in the collection; they need a meaning-based retriever.

STEP 7 · FROM SETS TO A RANKING

Ranking adds up matched words, and ties need a rule

Ranked keyword search reads the same postings but gives each document a score: here, the number of distinct query words it contains (BM25, in P3, weights each word instead). Two consequences follow. First, only documents in at least one postings list can score above 0; those are the retriever's candidates, and no k can reach past them. Second, equal scores need a tie rule, and the rule is part of the result:

Nothing about the documents changed, yet the relevant one moved from rank 2 to rank 1. The standard scoring tool, trec_eval, re-sorts each run by score and breaks ties by document id, so the order you wrote for tied documents is not necessarily the order that gets scored; tie order is an uncontrolled setting unless you give tied documents distinct scores or state the rule (Cabanac et al., CLEF 2010, external; which direction the tool breaks ties is reported differently by sources, UNVERIFIED).

Common mistake: "ties are rare, so the order doesn't matter." With small integer scores (word counts) ties are common, and the tie rule changes rank-based metrics.

In your project: break ties by chunk id and write the rule down (v0 does), so two runs give the same order.

STEP 8 · CUT AT K

Set precision and set recall

A RAG system passes the top k chunks to the LLM, so first treat them as a set R and compare it with the relevant set Rel from the qrels:

precision = | R ∩ Rel | / | R |  how much of what you returned helps
recall    = | R ∩ Rel | / | Rel |  how much of what helps you returned

For q1, Rel = {d01, d03, d09}. Raise k from 3 to 6 and watch the two retrievers differ:

q1k = 3k = 6
Keyword: precision / recall0.333 / 0.333only 3 candidates: 0.333 / 0.333
MiniLM: precision / recall0.333 / 0.3330.333 / 0.667 (d03 arrives at rank 4)
 measured · toy

Two warnings. Accuracy is useless here: almost every document is irrelevant to any one question, so a retriever that returns nothing is "right" about nearly everything. On the toy it would be right on 42 of the 50 pairs (84%), the 42 irrelevant ones. And recall needs the number of relevant documents, which you only know from your labels; step 10 shows what happens when the labels miss some.

Common mistake (MC-005): "a bigger k is always better" or "always worse". It raises recall only if the retriever has more relevant candidates to give, and it always adds noise and tokens.

In your project: RAG usually favours recall in the first stage, because the LLM can skim a few extra chunks but cannot use a chunk it never received; a reranker then restores precision (R3).

STEP 10 · THE TRAP IN EVERY TEST SET

Unjudged chunks count as misses

Nobody judges every pair in a real collection. Evaluation tools follow one convention: a document nobody judged counts as not relevant. That is fine when the labels found every relevant document. It is not fine when the labels came from one retriever's results, because another retriever's finds are then mostly unjudged.

Try it on the toy. Suppose labels had been written only for the documents in the keyword retriever's top 3 for each query (a "pool" built from one retriever), and both retrievers are scored at k = 4:

The same effect has been measured at scale. On the TREC-COVID collection, the BEIR authors counted how much of each system's top 10 had never been judged (Hole@10): 6.4% for BM25 but 30.6% for the dense retriever DPR. When they judged the holes, the dense model ANCE rose from 0.654 to 0.735 nDCG@10 and overtook BM25, which rose only from 0.656 to 0.668 (Thakur et al., NeurIPS 2021, external).

Three things to take from this:

  • Precision drops and recall rises, both falsely. Unjudged finds count as misses, and recall's denominator shrinks to the relevant documents that happened to be judged, so recall is overestimated. In the toy, 65% of MiniLM's top-4 slots are holes (Hole@4 = 1 − 0.350 = 0.650).
  • Check whether the holes decide the result. Score again with every hole counted as relevant; if the order of two systems flips between the two scorings, the labels, not the systems, decide the winner.
  • Fill the holes blind. Judge the unjudged top-k chunks without seeing which system found them, then make a new label version.

Labels are also noisy: two careful judges agree only fairly well. Agreement beyond chance is measured with Cohen's κ, where P(A) is how often they agree and P(E) how often they would agree by chance:

κ = ( P(A) − P(E) ) / ( 1 − P(E) )  above 0.8 good · 0.67–0.8 fair · below that, doubtful

Changing who judges usually changes the absolute scores much more than the order of the systems being compared.

Common mistake: "if a chunk isn't labelled relevant, it isn't relevant." It may simply never have been looked at. Report how much of each top k was judged (Judged@k, or its complement Hole@k) next to every metric.

Full · κ worked through 
IIR Table 8.2: two judges, 400 pairs: both yes 300, yes/no 20, no/yes 10, both no 70

In your project: when you label, pool candidates from both keyword and dense retrieval, judge every chunk in each run's top k, and report Judged@k beside every retrieval number.

STEP 12 · PUTTING IT TOGETHER

What this means for your test set

Documents, needs and labels together form a test collection, the design behind decades of search evaluation (the "Cranfield" approach, named after the first such experiments). The textbook rule of thumb is about 50 needs, tuned on one part (dev) and reported on another (test). The course follows it at a learning scale:

Idea from P2Where it lives in the course
Needs and queries06 eval items, each with a need note; at least 40 answerable and 6 unanswerable to start
Labels with a guidelineevidence spans (evidence_groups), written before any run is seen, guideline versioned (M1)
Chunk relevancederived from spans per chunking, by overlap (06 §7.2)
Holespool from keyword and dense retrieval; report Judged@k (M2)
Dev and testtune on dev, confirm once on test, keep a holdout (06 §9.4)

Small sets compare; they do not certify. With fewer than about 50 needs, the labels are still useful for comparing two versions of your system on the same questions, but weak for absolute claims such as "recall is 0.9".

Qrels measure retrieval only. A reference answer says what a good answer contains; it does not say which chunks hold the evidence, so it cannot score a retriever. The repo's data/q_a.json is exactly that: 44 question–answer pairs with no relevant documents. The book's own evaluation set pairs each question with its expected relevant documents and a reference answer, two different things (Ch22 p.12/19).

Common mistake: "my labels are complete because I labelled carefully." Careful labels of the wrong pool are still incomplete; the check is Judged@k on every run you compare.

The notebook study/notebooks/P2_ir_framing_verify.ipynb rebuilds the toy index, both retrievers, the labels, every metric and the PDF facts from code, checks each value against this page, and redraws the charts as interactive Plotly charts. It makes no API call.

  1. Open the notebook in VS Code (or from the P2 page of the study kit).
  2. Choose the kernel: Select Kernel → .venv (Python 3.12) of rag_learning.
  3. Run All. Each step prints its checks (✓ or ✗); the last cell prints the total.
What it rebuilds 95 checks · 10 interactive charts · about 15 seconds
  • Steps 1–4 rebuild the toy collection, both retrievers (keyword overlap and MiniLM, offline), the labels and the similarity-vs-grade chart for all 50 pairs.
  • Steps 5–7 rebuild the inverted index (and check it against a full scan), Boolean AND/OR with postings read, and the q4 tie flip.
  • Steps 8–10 recompute precision, recall and recall@k curves, runs A/B/C, and the one-retriever pool demo; BEIR's Hole@10 is drawn but labelled external.
  • Steps 11–12 rebuild the 91-window index of the climate PDF and turn the draft seeds' evidence spans into relevant windows.

A ✗ anywhere means the page and the code disagree: tell the tutor. Rebuild the notebook with uv run python sources/verification/P2/p2_build_verify_nb.py, then run it with p2_verify_nb_run.py.

APPLY · USE IT ON YOUR OWN DATA

Are your labels good enough to measure with?

The tool study/tools/qrels_check.py runs this lesson's checks on your documents, questions and labels: are they good enough to measure a retriever with? Run it from rag_learning/:

uv run python study/tools/qrels_check.py --docs "D:\my-project\docs" --questions q.jsonl --qrels labels.jsonl --dense --k 4 --report qrels_report.md
# labels as spans instead: --spans evidence.jsonl · your own runs: --runs run.trec · a blind second pass: --relabel labels2.jsonl
# see what a one-retriever pool hides: --pool-from keyword:3

It makes no API call. It builds its own keyword run from an inverted index and, with --dense, a MiniLM run on your machine. Labels can be JSON lines (qid, doc, grade, why, judge, guideline_version, ts), a TREC qrels file, or evidence spans in the course's evidence_groups form, which it turns into chunk labels by overlap. It also writes a clean qrels.trec file.

Two examples measured · toy and climate PDF

The toy, with a one-retriever pool (--pool-from keyword:3): it reproduces step 10 exactly. MiniLM: Judged@4 0.350 (Hole@4 65%), precision@4 0.350 → 0.200, recall 0.933 → 1.000; the keyword retriever's precision stays 0.433. Checklist ✗ on Q5, Q6, Q7 and Q9, so the recommendation is "pool is biased: judge the listed holes, then rerun", with the 13 holes listed.

The climate PDF, with the 10 draft seeds as spans (--spans study/tools/examples/climate_seed_spans.jsonl --dense --k 4): the index matches step 11 and the derived windows match its table. Spans label every chunk, so there are no holes. Checklist ✗ on Q3 (no guideline version on the seeds) and Q8 (keyword ties at the cut change precision@4 for D-004 and D-007); ? on Q9, because every OR set covers 80 to 91 of the 91 chunks. Recommendation: "labels not ready: fix the format and guideline first", which is right: all 10 seeds are LLM drafts you have not checked yet.
