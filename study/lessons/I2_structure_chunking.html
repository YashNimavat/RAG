RAG study kit · Phase 4 · concept I2

Structure-aware chunking

Cut a PDF where its author cut it: find the headings from the typography, build the section tree, and make chunks that follow sections across pages while staying exact slices of the frozen text.

measured from local runs on the course's climate PDF (PyMuPDF 1.26.7; no API call) · toy made-up data that shows a mechanism · formula computed on this page · external from a cited study or library source

THE ANSWER FIRST

Structure-aware chunking in five sentences

Structure-aware chunking cuts a document where its author cut it, at headings and sections, instead of every N characters or at every page break. A PDF stores no headings as such, so you recover them from the typography: a line larger than the body text is a heading, its size rank gives its level, and on the climate PDF a whole-bold line at body size is the next level down. The chunker pins every heading to an exact position in the frozen text, builds the section tree, makes one chunk per section, splits long sections into balanced pieces, and never lets a heading end a chunk, so every chunk stays an exact slice and span labels and citations keep working. On the climate PDF this repairs the 16 headings that v0 leaves alone at the bottom of a page and the 8 sentences it cuts at a page break, but 68% of its headings are bold body-size lines that a size-only detector misses. Whether it retrieves better is an experiment, not a promise: on today's 8 draft questions it is inconclusive, because none of them crosses a page or a v0 chunk boundary.

Next comes the whole theory in one read, then a formula sheet, then the step-by-step part, which follows the course's climate PDF (Corpus D, 33 pages, the frozen snapshot ea07572562ac) from raw PDF spans to 117 section chunks, and then measures them. Short gives a complete first understanding. Full adds other structure sources, what the frameworks really do, tables and layout models, and the published evidence; its parts have a dashed border.

THE THEORY IN ONE READ

The whole topic, idea by idea

Eleven ideas, in the order they build. This part is theory only, with no measurements. Each links to the step where the climate PDF shows it working.

  1. The PDF text model

    PyMuPDF reads a page as blocks, each block as lines, each line as spans: runs of text with one font, size and style. A span carries its text, its size, its font name and a flags number.

    page → blocks → lines → spans · span = (text, size, flags, font, bbox)
    Step 2 →

  2. Bold is one bit

    flags packs several yes/no facts into one number, one per bit. Bit 16 means bold. Fonts drawn bold without saying so set a bit in char_flags instead, so check both.

    bold(span) = (flags & 16) ≠ 0 or (char_flags & 8) ≠ 0
    Step 2 →

  3. The body size is the size with the most characters

    Most of a document is body text, so the size that holds the most characters is the body size. Count characters, not spans: many short spans (captions, labels) would otherwise outvote a few long paragraphs.

    b = argmax over sizes s of Σ len(text) of the spans with size s
    Step 3 →

  4. Heading levels from the typography

    Bold sizes above the body, from largest down, become levels 1, 2, 3. An all-bold line at body size becomes the next level. A numbering pattern ("Chapter 3:", "2.1") can give the level instead.

    level = rank of size among sizes > b · all-bold at b → next level
    Step 4 →

  5. Filters and joins

    A heading line is short (at most 12 words), has no final full stop, and is not repeated on 3 or more pages (a running header). Two heading lines of the same level in the same block are one heading that wrapped.

    words ≤ 12 · no final . ! ? · on < 3 pages · same level + same block → join
    Step 5 →

  6. Pin each heading to the snapshot

    Spans have no position in the frozen text. Walk the headings in reading order and search each one's line from a moving cursor; every heading must be found, or the build stops.

    pos = doc_text.find(line, cursor) · cursor = pos + len(line) · not found → stop
    Step 6 →

  7. The section tree

    A heading's section runs from the heading to the next heading of the same or a higher level. The titles of the open headings above it form its path (breadcrumb).

    section(h) = [start(h), start(next heading with level ≤ level(h)))
    Step 7 →

  8. Chunk at a split level; a heading never ends a chunk

    Pick a level L. Every heading at level L or above starts a new chunk, unless it directly follows another heading: then they travel together.

    boundaries = starts of headings with level ≤ L, except a heading right after a heading
    Step 8 →

  9. Split long sections evenly

    A section longer than the cap C becomes n pieces of about equal length, cut at a sentence end near each ideal point, never a full piece plus a scrap.

    n = ⌈ℓ / C⌉ · ideal cut points at ℓ·i / n · prefer a sentence end within ℓ / 2n
    Step 9 →

  10. Merge tiny neighbours

    Two neighbouring sections that are both below a minimum size, share a parent and fit the cap together become one chunk, marked as merged; its path is the parent's.

    merge if ℓ₁ < m and ℓ₂ < m and same parent and ℓ₁ + ℓ₂ ≤ C
    Step 9 →

  11. The invariants

    Whatever the rules, every chunk is an exact slice of the snapshot, no chunk is over the cap, chunks cover the text with no gaps, no chunk crosses a split-level boundary, and its page segments rebuild its range.

    doc_text[a:b] == text · b − a ≤ C · no gaps · no crossing · segments → [a, b)
    Step 11 →

STEP 1 · THE FAILURE IT FIXES

Where page cuts hurt

v0, the course's first index, splits each page on its own with the recursive splitter (1,000 characters, 200 overlap), exactly like the repo's encode_pdf (simple_rag.ipynb cell 10, helper_functions.py L48–L76). The book warns that chunks lose their headings and the context around them (Ch7 p.1–2/11; per 07, not re-opened in this session). On the climate PDF, page-by-page splitting hits two concrete failures:

The orphaned heading

Page 1 ends with the sub-heading "Coal"; its paragraph starts page 2. v0's last page-1 chunk ends with the word "Coal", and the chunk that explains coal has no heading. This happens at 16 of 32 page breaks.

The cut sentence

"Public-private partnerships (PPPs) can leverage resources, expertise, and innovation to implement" ends page 12; "large-scale climate solutions." starts page 13. No v0 chunk holds the whole sentence, and the 200-character overlap cannot help, because it never crosses a page. This happens at 8 page breaks.

Both counts were first measured on another parser's text; re-measured on the PyMuPDF snapshot the course uses, they are identical: orphans at 1→2, 2→3, … 31→32 (16), cuts at 12→13, 14→15, 15→16, 16→17, 21→22, 22→23, 24→25, 28→29 (8). Structure-aware chunking attacks the cause: the cut points ignore the document's own structure.

Common mistake: "more overlap fixes page cuts." Overlap repeats text inside one page's chunks; with per-page splitting it can never reach across a page break. Only changing where chunks may start and end helps.

In your project: this is project step v1.1 (06): the structure-aware chunker against v0 and the v1.0 champion from the I1 grid.

STEP 2 · LOOK INSIDE THE PDF

A PDF has no headings, only styled text

A PDF stores where to draw each piece of text and in which font, not what the text is. "Heading" is something a reader infers from size and weight. So the first job is to see the styling. Plain page.get_text() (what v0 and C1 use) throws it away; page.get_text("dict") keeps it, as blocks → lines → spans. A new span starts whenever the size, flags, font or colour change.

Here are the first three spans of page 1, exactly as PyMuPDF returns them (each span also has alpha, ascender, bidi, color, descender and origin):

textsizeflagschar_flagsfont
Understanding Climate Change24.002024TimesNewRomanPS-BoldMT
Chapter 1: Introduction to Climate Change18.002024TimesNewRomanPS-BoldMT
Climate change refers to significant, long-term …12.00416TimesNewRomanPSMT

flags is a bit field: one number that stores five yes/no facts, each as a power of two. You test one fact with a bitwise AND (&):

Bit valueMeansTitle (20)Body (4)
1superscriptnono
2italicnono
4serif fontyesyes
8monospacednono
16boldyesno

20 = 16 + 4, so the title is bold and serif; 4 is serif only. 20 & 16 = 16 (bold), 4 & 16 = 0 (not bold). PyMuPDF sets bit 16 from the font's own bold marker. A font that is drawn thicker without that marker does not set it; PyMuPDF then marks it in char_flags (bit 8), which is why the course's bold test checks both.

bold(span) = (span["flags"] & 16) ≠ 0 or (span["char_flags"] & 8) ≠ 0

Common mistake: "flags == 16 means bold." The title's flags are 20, not 16: other bits are set too. Test the one bit with &, never compare the whole number.

In your project: before writing any heading rule for a new corpus, print one real span dict per page type and read it.

STEP 3 · FIND THE BODY SIZE

The body size holds the most characters

Headings are defined relative to the body text, so you need the body size first. Add up the characters of every span, grouped by rounded size and boldness:

So b = 12: 12 pt holds 67,819 of the 70,368 counted characters (96.4%). Why count characters and not spans? A span is a run of one style, and its length varies wildly. toy A slide-like page with 40 one-word labels at 9 pt and 5 long paragraphs at 11 pt has 40 spans at 9 pt but only 5 at 11 pt; counting spans would call 9 pt the body. Counting characters (40 × 6 = 240 against 5 × 600 = 3,000) gives 11 pt, which is right. On the climate PDF both methods agree (12 pt also has 1,006 of 1,097 spans), but the rule has to work on PDFs you have not seen yet.

Common mistake: "the body is the smallest size." Footnotes, captions and page numbers are usually smaller than the body. The climate PDF even has a blank 11.04 pt Calibri span.

In your project: the apply-it tool prints this histogram per PDF, and its check S2 wants one size to hold at least half of the characters.

STEP 4 · DETECT THE HEADINGS

Headings by size, and then by bold

The usual rule, and the default in the pymupdf4llm PDF-to-Markdown converter, is size only: every bold size above the body gets a level, largest first. On the climate PDF that gives 24 pt → level 1 (the title), 18 pt → 2 (chapters), 13.56 pt → 3 (sections). The climate PDF has a fourth level, though: sub-headings like "Coal" or "Paris Agreement" are 12 pt bold, the body size, set apart only by weight. The course's rule adds one line: a line that is all bold at the body size is the next level.

RuleL1 titleL2 chaptersL3 sectionsL4 sub-sectionsTotal
Size only12168090
Size + bold at body size12168192282

Size only misses 192 of 282 headings, 68.1%. The four levels match the PDF's own structure: the research pass read Word's heading tags from the raw file (1 title, 21 chapters, 68 sections) and found the bold sub-headings untagged, so they are the deepest level (Full, step 14).

A numbering pattern is a third signal: ^Chapter \d+: or ^\d+(\.\d+)*\s gives the level from the depth of the number (2.1.3 → level 3). The climate PDF numbers only its chapters, so it confirms level 2 here and nothing else.

Before any of this, check for an outline. If the PDF has bookmarks, doc.get_toc() returns them as [level, title, page] rows: levels and titles the author chose. Use them first (still aligned to offsets, step 6) and fall back to typography only when the list is empty. The climate PDF's outline is empty ([]), so this lesson uses typography (Full, step 14).

Common mistake: "headings are always bigger than the text." Many Word, LaTeX and report templates set their lowest heading level in body-size bold, or even in body-size italic. Check a corpus before you trust a size-only detector.

In your project: the experiment card 06 v1.1 must say "size rank, plus bold at body size", and the apply-it tool reports the size-only vs size + bold difference as its check S4.

STEP 6 · PIN EACH HEADING TO THE SNAPSHOT

From a styled line to an exact offset

The course's chunks, gold labels and citations all use one coordinate system: character offsets into the frozen snapshot text, doc_text (C1; 06 §2.3). A chunk is a slice [a, b), and the page table says where each page starts. The span dicts from step 2 have no such offsets. They only have drawing positions on the page.

What saves us is a measured fact: on all 33 of 33 pages, joining the dict lines with a newline after each rebuilds page.get_text() character for character. Both views come from the same text extraction, in the same order. So each heading line can be found in doc_text:

cursor = 0
for h in headings:                        # in reading order
    pos = doc_text.find(h.line, cursor)   # search only after the previous heading
    if pos < 0:
        raise ValueError(f"heading not found: {h.title!r}")   # fail closed
    h.start, h.end = pos, pos + len(h.line)
    cursor = h.end

All 282 of 282 headings are found, each on its own page. "Coal" lands at [2418, 2423); page 1's text ends at offset 2424, so "Coal" is the last word of page 1, followed by the page join and then "Coal is the most carbon-intensive…" on page 2.

Why the moving cursor? Because of the repeated titles. A plain doc_text.find("Paris Agreement") for the p.23 heading would return the p.10 one, and the p.23 section would be built at the wrong place. Short titles also appear inside body text ("Coal" opens the very paragraph it heads). The cursor only searches after the previous heading, in reading order, so each heading finds its own copy. And if one is missing, the build stops instead of guessing: a chunker that silently drops a heading makes wrong chunks that every later metric would trust.

Common mistake: "convert the PDF to Markdown, chunk that, then find the chunks again." Converters rewrite the text (markup, joined lines, changed spacing), so their chunks are not slices of the snapshot and must be re-anchored, which can fail. LangChain's MarkdownHeaderTextSplitter also, by default, removes the heading from the chunk text (strip_headers=True, metadata only) and applies no size cap (Full, step 15).

In your project: the alignment rate is check S6 of the apply-it tool, and anything below 100% stops the build.

STEP 7 · THE SECTION TREE

Sections, and the path to each one

With levels and offsets, the document becomes a tree. A heading's section runs from the heading to the next heading of the same or a higher level (a smaller level number). The path is built with a stack: for each heading, pop every open heading at its level or deeper, then push it; the stack's titles are its path.

The two "Paris Agreement" sub-sections now have different paths:

p.10  Chapter 6: Global and Local Climate Action  > International Collaboration > Paris Agreement
p.23  Chapter 16: Global Cooperation and Governance > International Agreements    > Paris Agreement

Over the whole PDF: 21 chapters (1,591 to 6,058 characters, median 3,336), 68 sections (549 to 1,898, median 1,033.5) and 192 sub-sections (243 to 636, median 334).

Where does the path go? In v1.1 the chunk text stays the exact slice of the snapshot: it contains its own heading (the heading is part of the section's text) but not its parents' headings, which sit pages earlier. The path is stored as metadata next to the chunk, used for citations and debugging, and not embedded. Prepending it to the embedded text is a second change, so it is I5's experiment, not I2's. One change at a time.

Common mistake: "a structure-aware chunk carries its whole heading path in its text." Only its own heading is inside the slice. The parents' titles reach the embedder only if you add them, and that is I5.

In your project: the chunk record gets section_path, merged and page_segments fields; chunk_id and params_hash stay as in 06.

STEP 8 · CHOOSE THE SPLIT LEVEL

One chunk per section, at which level?

Pick a split level L. Each heading at level L or above starts a new chunk; deeper headings stay inside. One more rule: a heading never ends a chunk. When a heading is directly followed by another heading (a chapter title and then its first section title), no boundary is placed between them; they glue onto the text that follows. That is what fixes the orphans.

This lesson uses L3 (sections) as the main path: the sections average about 1,000 characters, close to v0's size, so the comparison changes the boundaries more than the size. 25 sections fit in one chunk; the other 46 are longer than the cap and become 2 chunks each. L4 makes chunks three times smaller, which changes two things at once.

A surprise: at L3, the size-only detector from step 4 gives exactly the same 117 chunks. The 192 bold sub-headings it misses are all inside sections, so they never decide a boundary at that level. They matter when you split at L4, and for the path.

Common mistake: "a structure-aware index has the same number of chunks, just better ones." The count changes (97 → 86, 117 or 209) and so does the size spread, so "top 4 chunks" means a different amount of text for each chunker. Step 13 compares at an equal text budget as well.

In your project: the split level is a parameter of the chunker config (split_level) and part of the index's params_hash; the apply-it tool's --split-level auto picks the deepest level whose median section is at least the minimum size.

STEP 9 · SPLIT LONG, MERGE TINY

Balanced pieces, and merged neighbours

Long sections. A section longer than the cap must be split, but not by filling 1,000 characters and leaving the rest. The shortest over-cap section, "Advocacy and Activism" (Chapter 11), has 1,004 characters: cap-filling would make a 1,000-character chunk and a 4-character scrap. The balanced rule first decides how many pieces are needed, then cuts them about equal:

n = ⌈ℓ / C⌉ = ⌈1004 / 1000⌉ = 2 · ideal cut at ℓ / 2 = 502 · cut at a sentence end within ℓ / 2n = 251 of it, else the nearest line break

The nearest sentence end gives 341 + 663. The longest section, "Greenhouse Gases" (Chapter 2, 1,935 characters), gives 990 + 945. Every piece fits the cap, and none is a scrap. The cut also may not fall inside or just after a heading, which keeps the "a heading never ends a chunk" rule inside long sections too.

Tiny sections. At L4 the sub-sections are 243 to 636 characters, so many chunks are small. The merge rule joins two neighbours when both are under the minimum m, they share a parent section, and together they fit the cap. With m = 400: 209 → 139 chunks, 70 of them merged, median 338 → 545. A merged chunk gets the parent's path (the sub-headings are inside its text) and merged = true.

Common mistake: "merging is the same as larger chunks." Merging only joins small siblings under one parent; it never glues the end of one section to the start of an unrelated one, which is what fixed-size windows do.

In your project: cap C = the v1.0 champion's size once I1 has run (1,000 until then); m is a project choice, so record it in the config and report the share of chunks under it (tool check S9).

STEP 10 · SECTIONS RUN ACROSS PAGES

One chunk, two pages, still citable

Because units are cut from the whole doc_text and not from single pages, a section that starts on one page and ends on the next becomes one chunk. Back to step 1's two failures, with L3 sections:

Textv0 (per page)L3 sections
"Coal" + "Coal is the most carbon-intensive…"split: chunk [1621, 2424) ends with "Coal"one chunk [1621, 2611), pages 1–2
The PPP sentence, doc [26866, 26997)split at the page break 26967whole, in chunk [26553, 27136), path Chapter 8 > Policy and Governance

27 of the 117 section chunks span two pages. A citation still needs pages, so each chunk carries page segments, one per page it touches. The newline that joins two pages belongs to the page before it (06 §2.3):

chunk [1621, 2611)  →  page 1: [1621, 2425)   page 2: [2425, 2611)
                        (page 1's text ends at 2424; 2424 is the join character)

The segments must rebuild the chunk's range exactly: the first starts at a, each ends where the next starts, the last ends at b. A citation can then say "pp.1–2" and still point to exact text.

Common mistake: "cross-page chunks break page citations." They break only if the chunk stores a single page number. Segments keep page-level citations exact.

In your project: v1.1 changes two things compared with a per-page v1.0 champion: the boundary rule and the page scope. So the experiment adds a control arm, the same recursive splitter run across pages (92 chunks, 41 on two pages), to tell the two apart (research error I2-E5). That control also keeps the PPP sentence whole: page scope alone repairs cut sentences, so v1.1 must beat it, not only per-page v0.

STEP 11 · THE INVARIANTS

Five properties every chunk must keep

Heading rules will change as you meet new PDFs. These properties must not. They are written as tests and run on every build, on the climate PDF and on a small set of made-up PDFs (the "zoo") that contain the error modes of step 5.

PropertyTestWhy
Round tripdoc_text[c.a:c.b] == c.textspan gold and citations only work on exact slices
Capc.b − c.a ≤ Cpredictable sizes; no surprise truncation
No gapsbetween neighbours, only whitespaceno text can vanish from the index
No crossingno split-level heading starts inside a chunk, unless only headings come before it, or the chunk is mergedthe whole point of the chunker
Segmentspage segments rebuild [a, b)page citations stay exact
def test_round_trip(chunks, doc_text):
    assert all(doc_text[c.a:c.b] == c.text for c in chunks)

def test_cap(chunks, cap):
    assert all(c.b - c.a <= cap for c in chunks)

def test_no_gaps(chunks, doc_text):
    assert all(not doc_text[x.b:y.a].strip() for x, y in zip(chunks, chunks[1:]))

Result on the climate PDF: 0 violations in every arm (L2, L3, L4, L4 + merge, and L3 with size-only headings). The L3 chunks also tile the document: no overlap, and only whitespace between neighbours.

Common mistake: "the retrieval metric will catch a broken chunker." A chunker that drops a section or shifts offsets can still score well on a small eval set; span gold is then compared with the wrong text. Test the properties directly.

In your project: these become T1 tests (07 H.3 covers only fixed and recursive chunkers today), plus a test that the heading counts per level equal the frozen outline (1 / 21 / 68 / 192), which catches a PyMuPDF upgrade that changes spans.

STEP 12 · LOOK BEFORE YOU MEASURE

Ten random chunks, read by eye

Before any metric, read a random sample: 10 chunks, a fixed seed (0), and a written verdict per chunk. Parsing errors show up here in seconds, and in a metric only as confusing misses. The sheet asks six questions per chunk: does it start at a heading or a clean continuation? Is its path right? Does it end at a sentence end (or was it forced by the cap)? Is there no foreign heading at its end? Is its length sensible? Are its pages right?

ChunkCharsPagesPath (chapter shortened)StartsEnds

measured · L3, random.Random(0).sample the verdicts are the tutor's reading in this session; filling in your own sheet is the notebook's task.

10 of 10 pass: each starts at a heading (two are second pieces of a long section that start at a sub-heading) and ends at a sentence end; 4 run across a page break. The research's rule is to stop and fix the parser if fewer than 8 of 10 pass: parsing errors would otherwise appear as false negatives in step 13.

Common mistake: "read the first ten chunks." The first pages are often the cleanest (title, introduction). A random sample with a fixed seed is fair and repeatable.

In your project: the apply-it tool writes this sheet (--sheet), and the eye inspection is a required step of the 06 v1.1 card.

STEP 13 · DOES IT RETRIEVE BETTER?

Measure it, and expect "inconclusive"

Better-looking chunks are a hypothesis. The test: embed each arm's chunks with MiniLM, retrieve for the 8 answerable draft questions, and score with the course's main metric, CE@4 (Complete Evidence: 1 if every evidence fact of the question is fully inside the union of the top 4 chunks, P6 D.7). Because the chunkers make chunks of different sizes, compare at a fixed k and at an equal text budget: take ranked chunks while their total length stays within 4 × v0's mean, 3,432 characters.

  • Sections (L3) are no better than v0 here: CE@4 8 of 8 for both; first-hit MRR 0.625 against 0.708.
  • Sub-sections (L4) rank single facts best (MRR 0.917: D-001, D-002, D-003 all at rank 1), because small chunks are focused. But L4 misses the two-fact question D-007 at k = 4: small chunks need more slots to hold two facts. At an equal budget it gets 8 of 8. That is why both comparisons are reported.
  • The decisive bucket is empty. I2 targets evidence that v0 cuts: spans that straddle a v0 chunk boundary, or cross a page. Of the 8 seeds, 0 straddle and 0 cross a page. These questions cannot show the gain I2 is built for.

The honest verdict is "inconclusive". To decide, the eval set needs items tagged straddles_champion (computed from the v1.0 champion's chunks before v1.1 runs) and crosses_page, frozen first; then v1.1 is compared with v0, the v1.0 champion and the cross-page control, at fixed k and equal budget, per bucket, reading 5 items that improved and 5 that got worse.

Outside this course, the evidence is mixed too: published comparisons find no consistent winner. Structure-aware chunking won in some domains, plain recursive splitting or one chunk per page in others, and the parser mattered as much as the chunker (Full, step 17; search-checked, UNVERIFIED).

When not to use it. Stay with the recursive splitter (the v1.0 champion) when a PDF has no text layer (scans), when its headings cannot be detected reliably (check S4 and the inspection sheet fail), when the reading order is wrong and cannot be fixed (columns), or when v1.1 shows no measured gain over the cross-page control. The other granularity choices have their own concepts: semantic chunking (I3), propositions (I4), and the comparison of all of them (I-CMP).

Common mistake: "structure-aware is the best choice whenever structure exists." Published comparisons disagree, and on these 8 questions it does not win. It is a candidate to measure, with a likely "inconclusive" on a small set.

In your project: M1 adds the boundary items before v1.1 is run; if v1.1 wins on them but loses on factual single-span questions, read the traces of the losers first: usually a chunk that got smaller, or a section split away from its key sentence.

STEP 16 · TABLES, COLUMNS AND LAYOUT MODELS

When typography is not enough

  • Tables must stay whole, and a table's rows must never be split from its header row; a row without its header is a list of numbers with no meaning. The climate PDF has none; the course's technical papers (Corpus T) do. unstructured and docling isolate tables by default; PyMuPDF has page.find_tables(). This belongs to C7 and S2.
  • Two columns: a text extractor can read across both columns line by line. Fix the reading order first (C1's column clipping by block x positions), then detect headings.
  • Layout models (docling's layout model trained on DocLayNet plus TableFormer; unstructured's hi_res) classify page regions as title, text, table, figure. They recover structure that typography hides, at the price of torch models and slower parsing. A later S2 decision.
  • Element-type chunking (Jimeno Yepes et al., 2024): chunk by element type from a document-understanding model; reported better than fixed token windows on financial reports, which are table-heavy.
  • Page-level chunking (NVIDIA's comparison): one chunk per page was best on average across five datasets. A cheap rival: v1.1 must also beat the best per-page arm.
