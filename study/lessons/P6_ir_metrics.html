RAG study kit · Phase 1 · concept P6

Retrieval evaluation basics

How to turn a ranked list of chunks into numbers you can trust: Hit@k, Precision@k, Recall@k, reciprocal rank, nDCG and the course's Complete-Evidence@k, what each one can and cannot tell you, and the quiet ways a metric lies.

measured from local runs with BM25 and MiniLM on the course's climate PDF (no API call) · toy made-up data that shows a mechanism · formula computed on this page · external from a cited study or documentation page

THE ANSWER FIRST

Retrieval evaluation in five sentences

To know whether a retriever got better, you fix the right answers in advance (labels), run every version on the same questions, and turn each ranked list into numbers. Hit@k asks whether any relevant chunk reached the top k, Precision@k how much of the top k is noise, and Recall@k what share of all the relevant chunks arrived. Reciprocal rank asks how early the first relevant chunk came, and nDCG how good the whole order is, giving more credit to more useful chunks. Each answers a different question: on the course's draft question D-007, which needs two facts, the local dense retriever gets Hit@2 = 1 while only one of the two facts is in its top 2, so the course's main metric, Complete-Evidence@k ("did all the evidence arrive?"), is 0 there. These numbers measure retrieval only, never the answer, and they are only as good as the labels: anything nobody labelled counts as wrong.

Next comes the whole theory in one read, then a formula sheet, then the step-by-step part. It follows one question from the course's draft test set, D-007 "What is the supreme decision-making body of the convention that provides the framework for negotiating the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement?", through the 97 chunks of the climate PDF (the course's v0 index, the same chunks as P3 and P4), ranked by two local retrievers: BM25 (keyword search, P3) and MiniLM (a small dense model, P4). Short gives a complete first understanding. Full adds the name clashes, library conventions, statistics, and the checks against the repo and the book; its parts have a dashed border.

THE THEORY IN ONE READ

The whole topic, idea by idea

Twelve ideas, in the order they build on each other. This part is theory only, with no measurements. Each idea links to its step below, where the climate PDF shows it working.

  1. A label is fixed before the run

    For each test question, a person decides in advance which chunks hold the answer. That decision is a label; a list of them is called qrels (query relevance judgements). A label is not a similarity score: it says what is right, not what looks close.

    qrel = (question, chunk, grade)  ·  grade 0 / 1 / 2  ·  relevant ⇔ grade ≥ 1  no label ⇒ grade 0
    Step 2 →

  2. Only the top k is scored

    The retriever returns a ranked list. Only its first k chunks are scored, because only they reach the language model. Every metric name carries its k: Hit@4, P@4, nDCG@10.

    top k = c₁ … c_k  k = the number of chunks the prompt receives (4 in v0)
    Step 2 →

  3. Hit@k: did anything relevant arrive?

    1 if at least one relevant chunk is in the top k, else 0. Its mean over questions is the hit rate.

    Hit@k = 1 if any relevant chunk has rank ≤ k, else 0
    Step 3 →

  4. Precision@k: how much of the top k is noise?

    The share of the top k that is relevant. Divide by k even when fewer than k chunks come back. With few relevant chunks, it cannot be high.

    P@k = (relevant in top k) / k  ·  P@k ≤ n_rel / k
    Step 4 →

  5. Recall@k: what share of the evidence arrived?

    The share of all labelled relevant chunks that are in the top k. With one relevant chunk it equals Hit@k.

    R@k = (relevant in top k) / (all relevant chunks for this question)
    Step 5 →

  6. Reciprocal rank and MRR: how early?

    One over the rank of the first relevant chunk, or 0 if none is within the cut-off. MRR is the mean of these over the questions; it is not one over the mean rank.

    RR@k = 1 / rank of the first relevant (0 if beyond k)  ·  MRR = mean of RR
    Step 6 →

  7. DCG and nDCG: the whole order, with grades

    Each chunk earns a gain from its grade, divided by a discount that grows with its rank. Dividing by the best possible score (every relevant chunk in the best order) gives a number from 0 to 1.

    DCG@k = Σ (2^gᵢ − 1) / log₂(i + 1)  ·  nDCG@k = DCG@k / IDCG@k  IDCG from all relevant chunks
    Step 7 →

  8. MRR and nDCG can disagree

    MRR sees only the first relevant chunk; nDCG sees all of them and their grades. One ranking can win on one and lose on the other.

    first hit only (MRR)  vs  every hit, weighted by rank and grade (nDCG)
    Step 8 →

  9. Counting chunks is not counting facts

    A question may need several facts. Complete-Evidence@k (CE@k), the course's primary metric, is 1 only if every fact is fully inside the top k. One chunk can hold two facts, and two chunks can repeat one.

    CE@k = 1 if every fact group is covered by the top k, else 0
    Step 9 →

  10. What k does

    Raising k never lowers Hit, Recall, reciprocal rank or CE. Precision can go either way, and nDCG can fall.

    k ↑ ⇒ Hit, R, RR, CE never fall  ·  P, nDCG can fall
    Step 10 →

  11. Unlabelled counts as wrong

    A chunk nobody judged scores as not relevant. If the labels were collected from one retriever's results, a different retriever's correct finds go unlabelled, and it is scored too low.

    unjudged ⇒ grade 0 ⇒ biased toward the retriever the labels came from
    Step 11 →

  12. Retrieval is not the answer

    These metrics say whether the evidence reached the prompt, not whether the answer was right. Pick the metric that answers your question, report one number per question and the mean with n, and keep answer metrics in a separate row.

    retrieval row: Hit, P, R, MRR, nDCG, CE  ·  answer row: correctness, faithfulness (later concepts)
    Step 12 →

STEP 1 · WHY THIS MATTERS

Two retrievers, one question: which did better?

D-007 asks which body is the "supreme decision-making body" of the convention behind the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement. The PDF answers it in two places: page 10 says the convention is the UNFCCC, and page 29 says its supreme body is the Conference of the Parties (COP). So the answer needs two facts from two chunks: s027 (page 10) and s085 (page 29). Here is what each retriever returns first:

2s027 p.10, the UNFCCC (fact 1)s085 p.29, the COP (fact 2)
3s085 p.29, the COP (fact 2)s017 p.6, climate policy (no)
4s084 p.29, policy instruments (no)s069 p.24, the Kyoto Protocol (no)
5s017 p.6, climate policy (no)s068 p.23, the Paris goals (no)

Which did better? If the prompt gets 4 chunks, both deliver both facts: on that question, at that k, they tie. If it gets 2, only BM25 does. If you care how early the first right chunk comes, BM25 is ahead (rank 1 against rank 2). "Better" is not one thing. Each retrieval metric is a precise answer to one of these questions, and the rest of the lesson builds them one at a time on this list. Slide k, or switch retrievers, and watch them move:

The point of P6: a retrieval metric is a precise question about a ranked list, so you choose the metric by the question you need answered, never by habit.

Common mistake: "the new retriever looks better on a few questions I tried." Looking is not measuring. Without fixed labels, fixed questions and a stated k, two people will read the same lists differently, and nobody can tell a real gain from noise.

In your project: every retrieval change in v0 and later is compared on the same labelled questions, at the same k, with the same metric code (06 §7).

STEP 2 · THE LABELS (A BRIDGE FROM P2)

Labels come first, and they are not similarity scores

Every metric on this page compares the retriever's list with a list of right answers decided in advance. P2 builds these in full; here is what P6 needs. For each test question, a person marks the chunks that hold the answer. Each mark is a qrel (query relevance judgement): a question, a chunk, and a grade.

QuestionChunkGradeWhy
D-007s027 (p.10)1overlaps fact 1: the UNFCCC frames the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement
D-007s085 (p.29)1overlaps fact 2: the COP is the UNFCCC's supreme body
D-007the other 950not labelled, so not relevant
 measured · K1 The course labels spans of the PDF (character ranges), and a chunk is relevant if it overlaps a span (06 §7.2). The draft seeds have no grades, so every relevant chunk here gets 1.

Four conventions, used everywhere below:

  • Grades. 0 = not relevant, 1 = partly relevant, 2 = holds the whole answer. For the yes/no metrics, a chunk is relevant if its grade is 1 or more.
  • Unlabelled = not relevant. Any chunk without a label counts as grade 0. Step 11 shows what that costs.
  • k is what the language model receives. v0 puts the top 4 chunks into the prompt, so k = 4 is the cut-off that matters for answers. Other k values are diagnostics, and every number names its k.
  • Only questions with an answer. Retrieval metrics are computed on questions that have at least one relevant chunk; the draft set has 8 such questions (D-001 … D-008). The 2 without an answer (D-009, D-010) are scored separately, by whether the system refuses.

Common mistake: "a chunk with a high cosine is relevant." A score is the retriever's guess; a label is the truth it is checked against. In step 1, the Kyoto chunk s069 has the highest cosine for D-007 (0.7094) and is not relevant: it names a different treaty.

In your project: the labels are spans in document offsets, not chunk ids, so re-chunking does not silently invalidate them (06 §7.2; the old lesson's trap J4).

STEP 3 · THE SIMPLEST QUESTION

Hit@k: did anything relevant arrive at all?

Hit@k is 1 if at least one relevant chunk is in the top k, and 0 otherwise. It is the yes/no question "could the model have seen any evidence?". Averaged over questions it is the hit rate: the share of questions with a hit.

Hit@k = 1 if some chunk with rank ≤ k has grade ≥ 1, else 0  ·  hit rate = mean of Hit@k

For D-007, the dense list misses at k = 1 (s069 is not relevant) and hits from k = 2 on. BM25 hits already at k = 1. Over the 8 answerable draft questions measured · K6:

             Hit@1        Hit@4
dense        4 of 8       8 of 8
BM25         7 of 8       8 of 8

At k = 4 the two tie on every question; at k = 1 BM25 is clearly ahead. P4 reported the same dense counts.

Common mistake: "Hit@4 = 1 means the evidence is there." It means some evidence is there. D-007 needs two facts, and dense Hit@2 = 1 with only one of them (step 9).

In your project: Hit@k at the prompt's k is the quickest health check for one-fact questions; for questions that need several facts it is only a first step.

STEP 4 · NOISE IN THE PROMPT

Precision@k: how much of the top k is noise?

Precision@k is the share of the top k that is relevant. The book's example: 3 relevant among 5 retrieved gives 0.6 (Ch22 p.4/19). A low precision means the language model reads irrelevant text next to the evidence.

P@k = (number of relevant chunks in the top k) / k

Two rules make it honest:

  • Divide by k, even when fewer than k chunks come back. A retriever that returns only 3 chunks for a k = 5 slot must not look better for returning less. In the old lesson's trap J1, dividing by the 3 returned gives 0.6667 instead of the right 0.4000 toy · S11.
  • Know its ceiling. If a question has only n_rel relevant chunks, P@k can never exceed n_rel / k. Most draft questions have one relevant chunk, so P@4 is at most 0.25 for them, however good the retriever.
ceiling and result, k = 4 (K5)    n_rel   ceiling   dense P@4   BM25 P@4
D-001, D-002, D-003, D-006, D-008   1      0.25       0.25        0.25
D-005, D-007                        2      0.50       0.50        0.50
D-004                               3      0.75       0.75        0.75

Both retrievers sit exactly at the ceiling on every draft question measured · K5, so on this set precision cannot tell them apart. A mean P@4 of 0.375 looks poor, but it is the best possible here.

Average precision (AP) adds order to precision. Take the precision at each rank where a relevant chunk appears, add them up, and divide by the number of relevant chunks. For a ranking "relevant, not, not, relevant, not" with 3 relevant chunks in the labels: (1/1 + 2/4) / 3 = 0.5000. Divide by the 2 relevant chunks found instead and you get 0.7500: that variant is what 06 and RAGAS call context precision measured · K8. The book's wording for AP's mean (MAP) does not say which denominator it means (Ch22 p.6/19), so always name it.

Common mistake: "P@4 = 0.25 means the retriever is bad." With one relevant chunk, 0.25 is perfect. Report n_rel or the ceiling next to every precision.

In your project: precision matters for cost and distraction (every irrelevant chunk is paid-for tokens the model must ignore), but with one or two relevant chunks per question it says little about quality.

STEP 5 · WHAT WAS MISSED

Recall@k: what share of the evidence arrived?

Recall@k divides by something different: the number of all relevant chunks in the labels, whether retrieved or not. It asks what was missed. The book's example: 3 of 10 relevant documents found gives 0.3 (Ch22 p.5/19).

R@k = (relevant chunks in the top k) / (all relevant chunks for this question)

For D-007 there are 2 relevant chunks. Dense: R@1 = 0, R@2 = 0.50, R@3 = 1.00. BM25: R@1 = 0.50, R@2 = 1.00 measured · K2, K3.

MetricDivides byQuestion it answersD-007 dense, k = 2
Hit@k(yes / no)did anything relevant arrive?1
Precision@kkhow much of the top k is relevant?0.50
Recall@kall relevant chunkshow much of the evidence arrived?0.50

When only one chunk is relevant, Recall@k equals Hit@k: the single chunk is either in the top k (1 of 1) or not (0 of 1). Five of the 8 draft questions are like that (K1).

The same word means other things elsewhere. When a vector database reports "recall@10 = 0.97", it usually means something else: how many of the exact nearest neighbours its fast approximate search kept (ANN recall, taught in P5). That number uses no labels at all, so it says nothing about whether the right evidence was found. Step 14 (Full) lists four meanings of "recall".

Common mistake: "Recall@k divides by k." That is precision. Recall divides by all relevant chunks, so a chunk that was never retrieved still lowers it.

In your project: for questions with several relevant chunks, recall tells you how much evidence was dropped; the course's CE@k (step 9) asks the sharper question of whether every needed fact arrived.

STEP 6 · HOW EARLY

Reciprocal rank and MRR: how early did the first right chunk come?

Reciprocal rank (RR) is one over the rank of the first relevant chunk: rank 1 gives 1, rank 2 gives 0.5, rank 3 gives 0.3333. If no relevant chunk is within the cut-off, RR is 0. MRR (mean reciprocal rank) is the mean of the RRs over all questions. The book's example: a first correct result at rank 3 gives 0.33 (Ch22 p.6/19).

RR@k = 1 / (rank of the first relevant chunk), or 0 if it is beyond k  ·  MRR@k = mean of RR@k over questions

Over the 8 answerable draft questions, with a cut-off of 10 measured · K6, K7:

first relevant rank   D-001 D-002 D-003 D-004 D-005 D-006 D-007 D-008     MRR@10
dense                   3     3     1     1     1     1     2     2       0.7083
BM25                    1     1     1     1     1     2     1     1       0.9375

Dense: (1/3 + 1/3 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 1/2 + 1/2) / 8 = 5.6667 / 8 = 0.7083. These are the same 0.708 and 0.938 that P3 and P4 reported. Notice that this is one number per question, then the mean, with n = 8 stated.

Two traps:

  • The mean of the reciprocals, not the reciprocal of the mean. On the toy questions with first ranks 2, 1 and 4, MRR is (0.5 + 1 + 0.25) / 3 = 0.5833, while 1 / (mean rank 2.3333) = 0.4286 is a different number toy · S6.
  • State the cut-off. On the same toy, a cut-off of 3 drops the question whose first hit is at rank 4, and MRR@3 = 0.5000. Some tools have no cut-off at all (step 13).

RR ignores everything after the first relevant chunk: a second relevant chunk at rank 2 or rank 50 changes nothing.

Common mistake: "MRR = 1 / average rank." It is the average of 1 / rank. The two differ whenever the ranks differ, and the trap is easy to write in code.

In your project: MRR@10 is the course's diagnostic for "how early" (06 §7.6). It suits one-answer questions; for questions that need several chunks, it hides the missing ones.

STEP 7 · THE WHOLE ORDER

nDCG: the whole order, with more credit for better chunks

Hit, precision and recall ignore order inside the top k; RR looks only at the first hit. nDCG (normalised discounted cumulative gain) scores every position, and can use grades. The book describes it in words: graded relevance, a gain per document, a logarithmic discount by rank, normalised by the ideal order, so the result is between 0 and 1 (Ch22 p.6/19). It gives no formula; the course uses this one (IIR §8.4; it comes from Burges et al. 2005):

DCG@k = Σi=1..k gain(gᵢ) / log₂(i + 1)  ·  gain(g) = 2^g − 1  ·  nDCG@k = DCG@k / IDCG@k

Three steps:

  1. Gain. Each chunk earns points from its grade. With the course's exponential gain, grades 0, 1, 2 earn 0, 1, 3, so a chunk that holds the whole answer earns three times a partial one. A linear gain (gain = g) would give 0, 1, 2.
  2. Discount. Divide each gain by log₂(i + 1). Rank 1 divides by log₂ 2 = 1, so it keeps its full gain; rank 2 keeps 0.6309, rank 3 0.5, rank 10 0.2891.
  3. Normalise. Divide by IDCG, the DCG of the best possible order of every relevant chunk in the labels, cut at k. A perfect ranking scores 1.

D-007, dense, by hand (binary grades, so both gains give 1 per relevant chunk) measured · K2:

k = 2:  DCG  = 0/1 + 1/log₂3          = 0.6309      (s069 not relevant, s027 at rank 2)
        IDCG = 1/1 + 1/log₂3          = 1.6309      (both relevant chunks, ranks 1 and 2)
        nDCG@2 = 0.6309 / 1.6309      = 0.3869
k = 3:  DCG  = 0.6309 + 1/log₂4       = 1.1309      (s085 at rank 3)
        nDCG@3 = 1.1309 / 1.6309      = 0.6934

BM25 puts both relevant chunks at ranks 1 and 2, the ideal order, so its nDCG is 1.0000 from k = 2 on (K3). With grades, the gain choice matters. The toy question Q1 has grades 2, 1, 1 at ranks 2, 4 and 7:

Common mistake: "build IDCG from the chunks that were retrieved." Then a ranking that finds only a partial chunk gets a perfect 1.0, because it is compared with itself. The right IDCG uses every relevant chunk in the labels: in the old lesson's trap J2 that is 0.2421, not 1.0 toy · S7.

In your project: report nDCG@10 with its gain named ("exp") every time; the same ranking gives a different number under the other gain. With binary labels, as on the draft seeds now, the two gains agree.

STEP 8 · TWO METRICS, TWO VERDICTS

When MRR and nDCG disagree

Two toy rankings of the same question, with three relevant chunks d1, d2, d3 toy · S8:

Ranking A
d1n1n2n3n4
P@5 0.2000   R@5 0.3333
RR  1.0000   nDCG@5 0.4693
Ranking B
n1d1d2d3n2
P@5 0.6000   R@5 1.0000
RR  0.5000   nDCG@5 0.7328

A wins on RR because its first chunk is right. B wins on nDCG, precision and recall because it delivers all three relevant chunks. Which is better depends on the question: one right document (a lookup) or all the evidence (a multi-fact answer).

The same happens on the real seeds measured · K12. On D-004, dense and BM25 both have RR = 1, but BM25's three relevant chunks sit at ranks 1, 2, 4 instead of 1, 2, 3, so its nDCG@10 is 0.9675 against 1.0000. On D-005, RR ties at 1 and nDCG is 0.9197 against 1.0000.

Common mistake: "one of them is the true score." They measure different things: MRR the first hit, nDCG every hit with its grade. A disagreement tells you how two rankings differ.

In your project: report MRR@10 and nDCG@10 side by side as diagnostics; when they disagree, read the two lists before deciding.

STEP 9 · DID ALL THE EVIDENCE ARRIVE?

Counting chunks is not counting facts: Complete-Evidence@k

Every metric so far counts chunks. An answer needs facts. The course's primary retrieval metric, Complete-Evidence@k (CE@k), asks: is every fact the question needs fully inside the top k? Each test question lists its facts as groups, each with the span of the PDF that states it (06 §7.2). CE@k is 1 only if every group is covered.

CE@k = 1 if every fact group's span is covered by the top-k chunks, else 0  ·  Group-Recall@k = groups covered / groups

Case 1: a hit, but half the evidence (D-007, dense, k = 2) measured · K2. Fact 1 (the UNFCCC) is in s027 at rank 2; fact 2 (the COP) is in s085 at rank 3.

D-007 dense   k = 2:  Hit 1   Recall 0.50   CE 0   Group-Recall 0.50     ← a "hit" with one fact missing
              k = 3:  Hit 1   Recall 1.00   CE 1   Group-Recall 1.00

A model given the top 2 cannot know that the COP is the body asked about. Hit@2 says "fine"; CE@2 says "incomplete".

Case 2: one chunk, two facts (D-005, k = 1) measured · K4. D-005 asks how the PDF contrasts coal and natural gas. Its two facts are close together on page 2, and chunk s003 (1,000 characters) runs from the coal sentence into the natural-gas sentence, so it covers both. s004 also overlaps the natural-gas fact. Both retrievers put s003 first.

D-005 dense   k = 1:  Hit 1   Recall 0.50   CE 1     ← half the relevant chunks, all of the facts

Recall says half the evidence is missing; nothing is missing. Two chunks are relevant and only one is needed.

So the three disagree in both directions. Hit@k can say "fine" when a fact is missing, and Recall@k can say "missing" when nothing is. CE@k is the one that matches what the language model needs. Overlapping chunks (v0 overlaps by 200 characters) make chunk counts even less reliable, because two chunks can repeat one fact.

Common mistake: "Hit@k = 1 on a two-fact question means the evidence is complete." Hit needs one relevant chunk; complete evidence needs every fact.

In your project: CE@4 is the headline retrieval number (06 §4.1); Hit, MRR and nDCG are diagnostics next to it. The real CE@k works on character spans, and M2 builds it; this page uses the course's reference code for it (contracts_check.ce).

STEP 10 · CHANGING K

What k does to each metric

Raising k adds chunks to the bottom of the list and never removes any. That fixes the direction of four metrics and leaves two free:

MetricWhen k growsWhy
Hit@k, Recall@k, CE@knever fallsa chunk already in the top k stays there
RR@knever fallsa first hit beyond the old cut-off now counts
Precision@kup or downthe new chunk may or may not be relevant, and the denominator grows
nDCG@kup or downIDCG grows too; it falls when a relevant chunk was never retrieved

On D-007 dense: precision is 0.50, 0.67, 0.50, 0.40 for k = 2, 3, 4, 5, while recall and CE reach 1 at k = 3 and stay there measured · K2. A toy case where nDCG really falls: one relevant chunk at rank 1 and a second one never retrieved gives nDCG@1 = 1.0000 but nDCG@2 = 0.6131 toy · S5.

Common mistake (MC-005): "sending more chunks is never good" or "always send more". A larger k can only help Hit, Recall and CE, while it lowers precision and adds tokens. The trade-off has two sides, and the right k is measured, not assumed.

In your project: compare retrievers at the k the prompt really uses (4 in v0), and show a small k sweep when choosing k.

STEP 11 · THE LABELS' BLIND SPOT

A chunk nobody labelled counts as wrong

Step 2's rule "no label ⇒ not relevant" is practical: nobody can judge every chunk for every question. But it is not neutral. Labels are usually collected by looking at what some retriever returned (a pool). A different retriever that finds a correct chunk outside that pool is marked wrong for it, and its score is too low. The missing labels are called holes.

The best-known measurement external · BEIR, Thakur et al. 2021, §6. On the TREC-COVID dataset, many chunks in each system's top 10 had never been seen by an annotator. After 980 of these pairs were judged by hand, the scores changed very differently:

The same effect, small, on the climate PDF measured · K9. Keep the labels only for the relevant chunks that BM25 put in its top 1, as if a labeller had read nothing else, and score both retrievers again (7 questions):

labels from BM25's top 1      full labels   →   pool labels
P@4    (both retrievers)        0.3929      →     0.2500
nDCG@10 dense                   0.7606      →     0.7517
nDCG@10 BM25                    0.9839      →     1.0000      ← the pool's own retriever goes UP
MRR@10 (both)                   unchanged (each first relevant chunk was in the pool)

For each retriever, four truly relevant chunks inside its top 4 were now scored 0. Precision fell for both. And nDCG of the retriever that made the labels rose, because the missing labels shrank IDCG too. On these 8 easy draft questions the effects are small, and their direction is not stable; BEIR shows how large they become on a real test set.

Common mistake: "a chunk with no label at rank 1 is just noise." It may be correct evidence nobody looked at. Unlabelled is "unknown", scored as "wrong", and that hurts whichever retriever differs most from the one the labels came from.

In your project: the course labels spans, so re-chunking cannot create holes. But a fact stated in a second, unlabelled place still can. Collect labels from a pool of both BM25 and dense results (M1), and hand-check every question where retrieval scored 0 but the answer was right.

STEP 12 · CHOOSING AND REPORTING

Which metric answers which question, and what none of them can say

Did any evidence reach the prompt?Hit@k at the prompt's k
Did all the needed facts arrive?CE@k (course primary)
How much of the prompt is noise?Precision@k, with n_rel
How early is the first right chunk?MRR@10
How good is the whole order, with grades?nDCG@10, gain named
What share of the relevant chunks arrived?Recall@k

A help desk with exactly one correct article per question needs only Hit@k and MRR: with one relevant item, Recall@k equals Hit@k and nDCG adds little. A multi-fact question needs CE@k.

Retrieval is not the answer. Every number here says whether evidence reached the prompt, not what the model did with it. The book separates the two: faithfulness checks the answer against the retrieved context, apart from retrieval quality (Ch22 p.8/19). A model can answer correctly with zero retrieval (from what it learned in training), or wrongly with perfect retrieval. So the report keeps two separate rows: retrieval metrics, and answer metrics (correctness, faithfulness), which later concepts build.

An LLM's "context relevance" score is a third kind of number. The book suggests having a language model rate each retrieved chunk (Ch22 p.4/19). The repo's evalute_rag.py goes further and has a model rate the joined top-k text on a 1–5 scale. Such a score has no labels, so it cannot say what was missed; joined text has no order; and the model's scale is not calibrated. In this course, model-judged scores run once at the end, on stored results, and are checked against human labels (D-10).

Report one number per question, then the mean, with n. With 8 questions, one question moving from rank 2 to rank 1 moves MRR by 0.0625 measured · K7. 06 works out that with 50 questions, if 10 of them change outcome between two systems, the 95% interval on the difference of a yes/no metric is about ±0.12 (06 §9.4). A small set shows a mechanism; it does not decide between systems.

Common mistake: "retrieval MRR went up, so the answers got better." Retrieval metrics bound what the model could use, not what it did. Check the answers separately.

In your project: the results table always has a retrieval row (CE@4, Hit@4, MRR@10, nDCG@10) and an answer row, each with n; the draft seeds give observations until M1 builds the real test set.

STEP 14 · NAMES THAT CLASH

Same word, different metric

Four things called "recall". When you read a number called recall, ask which of these it is:

NameDividesLabels neededWhere you meet it

RAGAS "context recall"claims of a reference answer supported by the retrieved text / claimsa reference answer; an LLM decides supportRAG evaluation frameworks

A vector database's "recall 0.97" is the third kind: it says the approximate search kept 97% of what exact search would return, not that 97% of the evidence was found. RAGAS also uses "context precision" for 06's AP-like formula, with an LLM deciding relevance, and its ID-based variant divides by the number retrieved, not by k.

STEP 16 · WHAT SMALL SETS HIDE

Noise, leakage, and the diagnostics next to CE@k

Why small sets are noisy. Reciprocal rank at cut-off 10 can take only 11 values (1, 1/2 … 1/10, 0). In the first evaluation campaign that used it widely (TREC-8 question answering, 2000), each question had only 5 answer slots, so it took 6 values, and the organisers doubted that parametric statistical tests suit it. On the 8 seeds, dense and BM25 tie on 3 questions, BM25 wins 4 and dense wins 1 (D-006) measured · K7. 06 §9.4 shows that even 50 paired questions detect only differences of about 0.18; M4 adds bootstrap confidence intervals.

Leakage. If test questions are written by reading chunks, the questions borrow those chunks' words, and keyword search looks better than it is. 06 §6.3 sets rules for writing questions from the document's spans instead; M1 builds the set.

Two diagnostics next to CE@k (06 §7.2, built in M2):

  • Group-Recall@k: the share of fact groups covered. D-007 dense at k = 2: 0.50 (one of two facts).
  • Span-Coverage@k: the share of the evidence characters covered, averaged over groups. It gives partial credit when a fact is cut by a chunk border. D-007 dense at k = 2: 0.5000 measured · K2.

Candidate pool vs final top k. A reranker (R3) re-orders a larger candidate pool, say the top 50, and keeps the top 4. Recall or CE on the pool is the ceiling the reranker can reach; MRR and nDCG on the final list say how well it ordered. Report both when there are two stages.

The draft seeds saturate. Both retrievers reach CE@4 = 8 of 8 and CE@3 = 8 of 8; only CE@1 (4 vs 6 of 8) and CE@2 (5 vs 8) separate them measured · K6. Harder questions are needed before any k = 4 comparison can mean anything (M1).
