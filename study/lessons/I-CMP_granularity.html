The granularity family, compared fairly

Fixed windows, structure, semantic breakpoints, propositions, headers and generated questions: what each one changes, what it costs, which failure it targets, and how to compare them without fooling yourself.

measured local runs on the course's climate PDF, no API call · toy made-up numbers that show a mechanism · formula computed on this page · derived a cost worked out from measured counts · external from a cited study

Read this first. None of the per-technique runs (I1–I6) exists yet. So this lesson measures what can be built offline on the climate PDF, calls those builds probes, and scores them on the 8 answerable draft questions, which an LLM wrote and you have not checked. Every rank or score on them is an observation, never a result. Where a real number is missing, the page says "not run", which is the honest answer.

THE ANSWER FIRST

The whole family in five sentences

Every member of the granularity family changes one of two things: where the boundaries go, or what text stands for a unit in the index. Boundaries can come from fixed windows (I1), the document's own headings (I2), jumps in meaning between sentences (I3) or facts an LLM writes out (I4); the stand-in can be the chunk plus a header (I5) or questions generated from it (I6), and a third choice, retrieving a small unit but handing back its parent, cuts across both. The members differ in their size unit, how much their sizes spread, what they cost to build (from 0 LLM calls to about one per fact), how big the index gets, and the failure they are meant to fix. None of them is better in general, and a fair comparison holds the questions, the labels, the embedder and the token budget fixed; with the 12 to 16 dev questions you will have, most differences will honestly read "inconclusive". So for a new corpus you match its structure and its questions to each member's target failure and cost, pre-register at most three arms, and write "not run" or "not comparable" wherever you have no fair number.

Next come the theory in one read, a formula sheet, and then twelve steps that follow the course's climate PDF (Corpus D, 33 pages) through every member that can be built without an API call. Short gives a complete first understanding. Full adds the repo and book checks and what lies beyond the family; its parts have a dashed border.

THE THEORY IN ONE READ

The whole topic, idea by idea

Eleven ideas, in the order they build on each other. This part is theory only; each idea links to the step where the climate PDF shows it working.

  1. Two axes plus one

    A boundary rule decides where units start and end. A representation rule decides what text is embedded for each unit. A third choice decides what is handed to the LLM: the unit itself or its parent. The three choices combine freely.

    unit = boundary(text) · pointer = representation(unit) · returned = parent(unit) or unit
    Step 2 →

  2. Name the unit

    "Size 500" means nothing until you say 500 of what. Characters, tokens of a named tokenizer, regex words, sentences and LLM-written facts are all in use; convert with a ratio ρ measured on your corpus.

    characters ≈ tokens × ρ  ρ per corpus and per tokenizer
    Step 3 →

  3. Look at the spread

    Two chunkers with the same mean size can differ wildly at the top. The embedder reads only its first L tokens, so the share of units over L is the share it reads only in part.

    truncation share = #(tokens > L) / N  L = 256 for MiniLM
    Step 4 →

  4. Index-time cost

    Count three things per member when the index is built: embeddings, LLM calls and the tokens those calls read. They range from N embeds and no calls to about one call per fact.

    cost(arm) = (E embeds, L LLM calls, T input tokens) per build × builds
    Step 5 →

  5. Query-time cost and index size

    At question time every member costs the same: one question embedding and a search, no LLM call. What grows is the number of vectors, and with it the bytes.

    bytes = 128 + N_vectors × d × 4
    Step 5 →

  6. Equal budget, not only equal k

    Four big chunks carry more text than four small ones, so "more found" may only mean "more read". Fill the same token budget B from each arm's ranked list instead, after removing duplicate parents, and make B at least as big as the largest unit.

    keep units in rank order while Σ tokens ≤ B
    Step 6 →

  7. A fair comparison

    Two rows may sit side by side only if they share every comparison key: the same questions, labels, split, corpus snapshot and scorer. Change one thing at a time, and report both fixed k and equal B.

    compare(a, b) only if keys(a) = keys(b)
    Step 7 →

  8. Honest cells

    A comparison table cell holds a measured value with its interval and n, or says why it does not: inconclusive, not run, not comparable, or a derived cost. The book's own ratings get a column of their own.

    Step 7 →

  9. Each member targets one failure

    Each technique is a hypothesis about one failure bucket (a boundary cut, a vocabulary mismatch, an anonymous chunk). Its row also says when not to use it.

    Step 8 →

  10. Conditional effects and family error

    Each v1.x arm is tried on top of the previous winner, so its effect is conditional on that base. Trying many arms on few questions makes a lucky winner likely; correct for it and know the smallest difference you can see.

    P(≥ 1 false win) = 1 − (1 − α)^m · Holm · MDE ≈ 2.8 · √(b + c) / n
    Step 9 →

  11. Choosing

    Rank candidate arms by how often their target failure occurs, how bad it is, and what the arm costs. Other people's benchmarks transfer as directions and design rules, not as numbers to rank against yours.

    priority ≈ bucket count × severity / cost
    Step 11 →

STEP 2 · THE MAP

Two axes and a return arrow

Sort the six techniques by what they change. Four of them move the boundaries: fixed windows (I1), the document's headings (I2), drops in similarity between neighbouring sentences (I3), or facts an LLM writes out (I4). Two leave the boundaries alone and change the representation, the text that is embedded for each unit: a header in front of it (I5), or questions written about it (I6). Because the axes are independent, members compose: structure-aware chunks with a heading-path header is one arm, not a contradiction.

The third choice is what you hand to the LLM. The unit you search with can be smaller than the unit you return: a generated question finds its parent chunk (I6), a proposition can point back to the chunk it came from (I4), and later the context-window step returns the neighbours of a hit (X1). "Retrieve small, return big" gives precise matching and readable context; its catch is that two small hits often share one parent, which step 6 deals with.

Common mistake: "semantic chunking replaces headers." They act on different axes; you can test semantic boundaries with or without a header.

In your project: each arm's record names its boundary rule, its representation rule and its returned unit, so a reader can tell which axis an arm moved.

Common mistake (MC-001): "a token is about a word, so 1,000 tokens ≈ 1,000 characters." On this PDF a v0 chunk of 1,000 characters is about 150 WordPiece tokens; setting a "1,000-token" chunker gives units more than five times larger.

STEP 4 · LOOK AT THE SPREAD, NOT THE MEAN

Size spreads, and what the embedder cannot read

Seven arms were built on the climate PDF with no API call: v0 (recursive, 1,000 characters, overlap 200, per page), three more points of the I1 grid, one unit per page, one unit per section (cut at chapter and section headings, no size cap), and an I3 probe: semantic breakpoints with MiniLM over the 612 sentences, threshold at the 90th percentile of neighbour distances, no cap. Each dot below is one unit, measured in MiniLM tokens.

ArmUnitsCharacters min / median / maxTokens median / maxOver 256 (cut)
 measured · climate PDF MiniLM reads the first 256 tokens; it was trained on 128.

Three things stand out. Pages are 97% truncated on MiniLM: the embedder never sees most of each page. Uncapped semantic chunks run up to 4,029 characters, because nothing stops a long stretch of similar sentences from becoming one chunk. And headings make tiny units: the section arm's smallest unit is a 30-character heading with no body. A size table that shows only the mean would show none of this.

Common mistake: "the average chunk is under the limit, so the embedder reads everything." The average says nothing about the tail; count the units over the limit.

In your project: each arm reports min, median, p95 and max in characters and embedder tokens, plus the truncation share, before any retrieval number. An arm with a large truncation share needs a cap or a longer-context embedder first.

STEP 5 · INDEX-TIME COST, QUERY-TIME COST, INDEX SIZE

What each member costs

All members cost the same when a question arrives: one question embedding and a search, with no LLM call. They differ when the index is built, and every rebuild pays again. Counted on the climate PDF (N = 97 v0 chunks):

The LLM context arm has a size problem as well as a cost. Each of its calls carries the whole document (13,026 cl100k tokens) plus the chunk and an instruction, about 13,280 tokens in one request. A free plan limited to 8,000 tokens per minute cannot accept that request at all. The money is small (about $0.10 at the course's Groq price, $0.13 on Claude Haiku 5.5); the request size decides.

Index size follows the vector count: bytes = 128 + N × d × 4. v0 with 1,536-number vectors takes 596,096 bytes; HyPE with 8 questions per chunk keeps 776 vectors, eight times as many.

Common mistake (MC-003): "HyPE makes every question slower because it calls an LLM." HyPE's calls happen at index time; at question time it is one embedding and a search, like v0. HyDE (Q2) is the one that calls an LLM per question.

In your project: each arm's row carries its index-time calls, input tokens, embeds, vectors and bytes per build, and the cost per month at your rebuild rate.

STEP 6 · COMPARE AT THE SAME TOKEN BUDGET

Equal k or equal budget

Score every arm at k = 4 and the bigger units win on coverage for a dull reason: four pages carry far more text than four small chunks. So fix the budget instead. Here B is the average size of v0's top 4 on the 8 draft questions, measured with the generator's tokenizer: B = 671 tokens. Each arm then walks its own ranked list and keeps units while the running total stays within B, stopping at the first unit that does not fit (the course's take_within_budget).

ArmContext tokens at k = 4Complete evidence at k = 4Mean k at BComplete evidence at B
 measured "Complete evidence" = every evidence span of the question is inside the kept units (CE, P6). The draft questions saturate v0 at 8 of 8, so no arm can look better than v0 here.

Two traps show up in the numbers:

  • B must be at least the largest unit. The I3 probe's largest chunk is 769 tokens, bigger than B. For one question (D-001) it ranked first, did not fit, and the rule stopped: zero units kept. State the rule (stop, or skip and continue) before you run, and check B ≥ the largest unit of every arm.
  • Overlap and duplicate parents spend the budget twice. v0's top 4 hold 671 tokens but only 3,412 distinct characters, because neighbouring chunks share up to 200 characters. With "retrieve small, return big" it is worse: two questions that point at the same parent bring it twice. Remove duplicate parents before filling the budget.

Common mistake (MC-005): "bigger chunks are better because they found more evidence at k = 4." They also sent more text. At an equal budget the 2,000-character arm drops from 7 to 4 of 8 here.

In your project: report every arm at fixed k and at equal B, with the tokenizer named, the realized tokens per question (mean, min, max) and the distinct characters in context.

STEP 7 · WHAT A CELL MAY SAY

Fill the table honestly

The outcome of this concept is a filled comparison table. "Filled" does not mean every cell holds a number. A cell holds one of these:

Cell stateWhat it containsWhen

Not comparable is a decision, not a gap. The v1.4 plan runs propositions on a 5-page subset, so its questions are a subset of v1.3's. The course's comparison check refuses to put the two side by side: on the draft questions, the full set against a 3-question subset is refused with "comparison key differs: qids", and the subset against the champion re-run on the same 3 questions is accepted (questions are compared as sets). So v1.4's row reads "not comparable: qids" until the champion is re-run on the subset's questions.

Common mistake: "leave the cell blank if the arm was not run." A blank reads as a zero or as forgotten work. Write the state and the reason.

In your project: the 06 roll-up needs more columns than it has (index calls and tokens, vectors and bytes, base champion, k and B, split and n, qid-set hash, and "comparable to"); add them before v1.0 runs.

STEP 8 · EACH MEMBER IS A HYPOTHESIS ABOUT ONE FAILURE

Which failure each member targets

Phase 3's failure log (M5) sorts misses into buckets. Each member of the family is a bet on one of them, and each has a case where it should not be used:

hypothesis ↑ expected to help · ↓ expected to hurt · blank no effect expected. Every measured cell reads "not run" until the per-technique runs exist; then each cell gets a Δ with its n.

Corpus D cannot test a title header. The climate PDF is one document, so a header holding the document title is the same on all 97 chunks: 1 distinct value of 97. It shifts every vector the same way and carries no information to rank by. A path header (title, chapter, section at the chunk's start) has 71 distinct values of 97, adds about 10% to each chunk's tokens and pushes none over 256. Title headers need a corpus with many documents (Corpus T).

Structure is there, but not where a size rule looks. The PDF has 282 headings: 1 title, 21 chapters, 68 sections and 192 sub-headings. The 192 sub-headings (68% of those below the title) are bold text at body size, so a detector that only looks for larger fonts misses them. And 11 heading names repeat across chapters ("Paris Agreement" on pages 10 and 23): only the chapter level tells those pages apart.

Common mistake: "a technique that helps on average should be adopted." Read the bucket: a header arm may help near-duplicate questions and do nothing for the rest. The book's Low / Medium / High ratings are hypotheses with no scale, not measurements.

In your project: write the bucket hypothesis for each arm before it runs, report Δ per bucket with n, and fill the "when not to use" column with one example from your own documents.

STEP 9 · MANY ARMS, FEW QUESTIONS

The family needs its own statistics

Effects are conditional. The v1.x plan tries each arm on top of the current champion: v1.1 on v1.0's winner, v1.2 on v1.1's, and so on. So "headers add +x" really means "headers add +x on top of structure-aware chunks". On v0 the same arm could do more, less or nothing. Every row records its base.

Trying many arms makes a lucky winner likely. If each comparison has a 5% chance of a false "win" and none of the arms truly helps, the chance of at least one false win after m comparisons is 1 − 0.95^m:

Two defences. Cap each round at three pre-registered arms, and correct within the round with Holm: sort the p-values, compare the smallest with α/3 (0.0167), the next with α/2 (0.025), the last with α (0.05), and stop at the first that fails. Holm never rejects less than Bonferroni (α/3 for all three) and often more.

Here b counts questions only the first arm gets right and c questions only the second gets right; only those disagreements carry information. Dev will hold about 12 to 16 answerable questions (30% of 40–55), so "inconclusive" is the expected verdict for most rows, and writing it is a success, not a failure.

The draft questions show this in miniature. On them no arm beats v0 on a single question (c = 0 everywhere). Pages lose 3 to 0 at k = 4; a bootstrap interval excludes zero, yet the exact two-sided test on 3 disagreements gives p = 0.25, and at equal budget the strongest loss (6 to 0, p = 0.031) still misses Holm's first threshold across 6 arms (0.0083). On 8 questions a bootstrap is overconfident; count the disagreements.

Common mistake: "the arm with the best dev score wins." With many arms and a small dev set, the best dev score is partly luck; it needs a pre-registered shortlist, Holm, and one confirmation on test.

In your project: keep one register of every arm tried (the denominator for the family error), grow the eval set before v1.0, and break ties towards v0 or the cheaper arm.

STEP 10 · OTHER PEOPLE'S BENCHMARKS

Directions transfer, numbers do not

Published chunker comparisons use different scores, k, corpora, embedders and question sources. A number from one cannot be ranked against a number from another, or against yours. What each one does give you is a direction under stated conditions, or a design rule:

How can two reports both be right? Character-range recall rewards returning more text; IoU penalises every returned character that is not gold. At a fixed k, a chunker with bigger units wins on recall and loses on IoU on the same data. Neither number is wrong; they answer different questions, and neither fixes the amount of text sent.

Common mistake: "their chunker scored 0.9, ours 0.6, so theirs is better." Different score, k, corpus and questions: the two numbers are not on one scale.

In your project: quote outside results only as "direction, under these conditions", and turn them into hypotheses for your own table.

STEP 11 · CHOOSING FOR A CORPUS YOU HAVE NOT SEEN

A decision procedure, worked once

For a new corpus, answer five questions in order. Each one turns a member on or off:

Worked example. An HR policy handbook: 120 Markdown pages with clean H1–H3 headings, three near-identical regional versions (UK, US, India), questions such as "how much parental leave do I get in the US office?", updated every month.

  • Structure exists, so boundaries come from the headings (I2).
  • Near-duplicates exist (three regions), and that is exactly where headers help: prepend region, document title and section path (I5 from headings, 0 LLM calls). The interview repo's own example of a heading path is "Handbook > Benefits > Parental leave" (AIQ 04 Q4, MIT, © 2026 Om).
  • Skip I3: the headings already give the boundaries, and the studies in step 10 predict little gain on real documents.
  • Skip I4: a monthly rebuild would pay its LLM calls every month, and the questions are lookups a section already answers.
  • Keep I6 for later, only if a vocabulary-mismatch bucket appears in the failure log.
Pre-registered plan
  • A0 recursive 1,000 / 200 (baseline) · A1 = I2 (heading sections, capped at the embedder's limit) · A2 = I2 + I5 (region + title + section path).
  • Primary: complete evidence at k = 4 and at an equal budget B, on region-scoped questions. Guards: MRR@10 and mean context tokens.
  • Two comparisons (A1 vs A0, A2 vs A1) with Holm at 0.025 / 0.05; select on dev, confirm once on test; MDE stated at the dev n; "inconclusive" allowed; ties go to the cheaper arm.

Common mistake: "test every technique to be safe." Every extra arm raises the family error (step 9) and the cost; three pre-registered arms with reasons beat ten tried at random.

In your project: the same five questions decide v1.x on Corpus T; Corpus D keeps parity with the repo. Never use Corpus H for this exercise: it is kept for the capstone (K1).

STEP 15 · BEYOND THE FAMILY

Late chunking, auto-merge, and two finer points

  • Late chunking moves along the representation axis without an LLM: embed the whole document with a long-context model, then average the token vectors inside each chunk's span, so each chunk's vector has seen its context. It needs an embedder that returns token vectors and reads the whole document; Cohere's API returns one vector per text, so it is a demo, not a v1.x arm.
  • Hierarchical chunking with auto-merge (HiChunk): chunk at several levels and, when enough children of one parent are retrieved, return the parent. It is "retrieve small, return big" decided at query time.
  • Chroma's precision denominator adds up the lengths of the retrieved chunks without merging overlaps, so overlapping chunks are penalised twice; its "precision Ω" is the best score any set of chunks could reach. Read their numbers with that in mind.
  • The crossover with caching: with the course's record-and-replay cache, re-running an evaluation costs neither I6 nor Q2 anything; the crossover in step 5 matters for real traffic and for rebuilds after the corpus changes.
  • When Corpus T and Corpus D disagree about an arm, that is a finding: write it down with both n, rather than averaging the two.

APPLY · USE IT ON YOUR OWN DATA

Which arms should you test, and what will they cost?

The tool study/tools/chunker_chooser.py runs this lesson's checks on your documents and questions and drafts a pre-registered plan. Run it from rag_learning/:

uv run python study/tools/chunker_chooser.py --docs "D:\my-project\docs" --questions q.jsonl --report chooser_report.md
# a usage profile (rebuilds and questions per month, budget, provider limits): --profile profile.json
# the embedder whose cap matters: --embedder minilm (256) or cohere-v4 · your dev size for the MDE: --dev-n 12

It makes no API call and needs no embedding model: it measures the text (structure, sizes against the cap, near-duplicates, anonymous chunk starts, the question mix) and estimates each arm's index-time cost from token counts.
