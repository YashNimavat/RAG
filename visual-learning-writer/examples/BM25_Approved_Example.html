Why the difference? Dense search compares meaning in an embedding space. BM25 compares matching tokens and how informative they are. The two systems notice different kinds of evidence.
02 / The intuition

BM25 is keyword search with three sensible adjustments.

Not every word match deserves the same score. A rare name carries more information than a common word; repeated mentions help, but less each time; and a match in a short chunk often matters more than the same match in a long one.

What TF-IDF means in ordinary words

TF-IDF stands for term frequency–inverse document frequency. It rewards a word when it occurs often in this chunk, but gives that word more importance if it occurs in few chunks overall.

TFFrequency inside a chunk

If bpa appears twice in one passage, its term frequency there is 2.

IDFHow rare across the collection?

If bpa occurs in only 2 of 6 passages, a match is more informative than a word appearing almost everywhere.

TF-IDF combines them. One more mention increases the score; matching a rare word increases it more than matching an ordinary word. This is why it is better than counting words alone.

Now the reason BM25 exists: simple raw-count TF-IDF keeps giving full credit for every repetition. A passage saying “BPA BPA BPA...” can get a large score even if another passage better covers the complete question. BM25 makes repeats count less and adjusts for chunk length.

Work through a tiny TF-IDF calculation

The textbook raw-count version in your P3 file uses the following formula. Other TF-IDF implementations may add smoothing or vector normalization.

For one query word in one chunk
TF-IDF(t, d) = TF(t, d) × ln(N / DF(t))
N = total chunks · DF(t) = chunks containing the word · ln = natural logarithm

Take the same toy collection: 6 documents, of which 2 contain bpa. In our example chunk, bpa appears twice.

Important distinction: the raw IDF in this textbook TF-IDF formula is ln(N / DF). The specific rank_bm25 library uses a different IDF formula and an additional adjustment for negative values. They are related ideas but not identical numeric weights; we cover the library's formula below.

Common misconception: “BM25 and TF-IDF are the same except for length.” Not quite. BM25 also replaces the linear repetition reward with diminishing returns, and its IDF implementation can differ.

04 / From TF-IDF to BM25

TF-IDF keeps growing. BM25 makes repetition flatten out.

Suppose someone searches for “BPA ban”. Passage A mentions each word once. Passage B repeats “BPA” six times but never mentions “ban”. As we just saw, raw-count TF-IDF gives every repetition full credit. Passage B can therefore win for the wrong reason.

BM25 uses saturation: the first few matches help the most. Later repetitions add smaller and smaller increments. This does not prevent every bad ranking, but it helps avoid blindly rewarding repeated words.

05 / The actual mechanism

The formula is just those three ideas multiplied together.

The book explains BM25 in words. Your P3 lesson verifies the specific implementation in rank_bm25 version 0.2.2, which we use here. For each query token t and document d:

Per-token score (sum this over all query tokens)
score(t,d) = IDF(t) × [ f × (k₁ + 1) / ( f + K ) ]
K = k₁ × (1 − b + b × L / avgL)
f = frequency in chunk · L = chunk length · avgL = average chunk length

The IDF handles rarity. The fraction handles diminishing reward for repeats. Inside that fraction, K handles chunk length. Nothing in the formula represents semantic meaning.

Other query words each contribute their own value. BM25 adds those contributions to get the document score and then ranks documents by that score.

A library detail worth remembering

rank_bm25 computes raw IDF as ln((N − n + 0.5)/(n + 0.5)), with N total chunks and n containing the word. If raw IDF is negative, the library replaces it with 0.25 × the mean raw IDF across its vocabulary. That replacement is called its floor.

Why this matters

“Common words have zero score” is not always true in this implementation. A term occurring in exactly half the documents has raw IDF zero; a term occurring in more than half is floored and can receive a nonzero score. The mean, and therefore the floor, can even be negative on unusual tiny or repetitive collections. Don't interpret BM25 scores as confidence percentages.

06 / The easy-to-miss failure

A perfect formula cannot match a broken token.

Before scoring, BM25 turns the query and each passage into tokens. With Python's basic split(), punctuation and capitalization stay attached: BECCS and (BECCS), are different strings.

The P3 demonstration is particularly striking: with the repo's unnormalized split(), the BECCS question matched zero of 91 overlapping windows. With normalized tokens, the windows containing BECCS ranked at the top.

What this means for your code: tokenize the index and query consistently. Test codes, acronyms, punctuation and numbers explicitly. Even a zero-score retriever may still return arbitrary top-k chunks unless your application filters them.

Normalization itself needs care. A tokenizer that splits 1.5 into 1 and 5, or breaks ERR_CONN_RESET into pieces, may harm identifier search. Your source uses a regex that preserves decimal numbers and comma-separated digits; that is still only one choice, not a universal tokenizer for every domain.

08 / Apply the idea

Use BM25 for exact names. Use dense retrieval for meaning. Test both.

BM25 has no built-in understanding that two different words can mean the same thing. Dense retrieval can bridge wording differences, but it may under-rank a rare identifier. That is why a hybrid retriever can be useful.

If your users ask…A sensible retrieval experiment
“Where is ERR_CONN_RESET documented?”BM25, with tokenizer tests for codes
“How can we reduce carbon from energy?”Dense retrieval for paraphrases
Mixed exact terms and natural-language questionsCompare BM25, dense, and hybrid

What can still go wrong?

A high BM25 score means strong lexical overlap under that scoring system, not that the passage actually answers the question. A vague question can match many chunks and receive a higher raw score than a more useful passage. Very small or near-duplicate indexes can also behave oddly with the specific IDF floor used by rank_bm25. And hybrid search is not automatically better: it needs evaluation on real queries.

For your RAG project, build evaluation groups for exact identifiers, paraphrases, and mixed questions. Track whether each method retrieves supporting evidence in the top-k, rather than comparing raw BM25 scores to vector similarities.

The idea to remember

BM25 ranks matching tokens by rarity, repetition (with diminishing returns), and document length. Dense retrieval ranks semantic similarity. The tokenizer decides whether BM25 gets to match a word in the first place.

09 / One-minute understanding check

Can you use the idea in a new situation?

No need to memorize the formula. Try these without looking back—including the TF-IDF idea that leads into BM25.

Question 1 · Concept

In BM25, a word appears in just 2 out of 100 chunks. What does that usually mean?

IDF uses how many chunks contain the term. A word found in only a few chunks is often a strong locator.

Question 2 · Debugging

Your PDF contains (IPCC) but the query IPCC retrieves no keyword match. What should you check first?

If query and document create different tokens, adjusting the BM25 formula can't create a match.

Question 3 · Connecting prior concepts

Why does the raw-count TF-IDF score keep growing when a word repeats, while BM25's frequency contribution flattens?

The simple TF-IDF formula uses raw TF as a direct multiplier; BM25 still rewards a repeated query term but each extra mention contributes less.

Question 4 · Mechanism

With b = 0, what happens to BM25's chunk-length adjustment?

At b = 0, the length multiplier becomes 1. Repetition and IDF still matter.
